## B8. Low Back: Non-specific LBP

> **Format note:** This protocol applies the standard Module 2.3 7-14-28 day phase framework to non-specific low back pain (NSLBP) — the most common musculoskeletal presentation in outpatient physiotherapy worldwide. Non-specific LBP is defined as pain in the lumbosacral region without a specific identifiable pathoanatomical cause (disc herniation, fracture, infection, malignancy, inflammatory arthritis, or radicular syndrome). It is a diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out red flags (B8.12) and specific pathology (B9-B12). The term "non-specific" does not mean "untreatable" — it means the precise tissue source cannot be confidently identified, but the condition remains highly responsive to an active, movement-based, education-led physiotherapy approach. This protocol is the foundation from which all other low back protocols (B9-B12) diverge once a specific pathoanatomical driver is identified.

### B8.1 Anatomy Link

The lumbar spine comprises five vertebrae (L1-L5), with the lumbosacral junction (L5-S1) as the most mobile and most commonly symptomatic segment. Non-specific LBP can arise from any of the following structures, often in combination:

- **Intervertebral discs:** the annulus fibrosus is innervated in its outer third; discogenic pain is typically deep, central, or paramedian, and aggravated by sustained loading (sitting, forward flexion) and relieved by positional change. Unlike lumbar disc herniation (B9), there is no radicular component — the pain remains axial.
- **Zygapophyseal (facet) joints:** paired synovial joints between adjacent lumbar vertebrae; facet-mediated pain is typically unilateral or paramedian, aggravated by extension, rotation, and rising from flexion, and relieved by flexion. Pain may refer to the buttock or posterior thigh but does not extend below the knee (that would suggest radiculopathy, B9).
- **Sacroiliac joint (SIJ):** pain localized to the posterior pelvis, aggravated by sitting, rising, and unilateral weight-bearing; managed separately when dominant (B11), but often co-present in NSLBP presentations.
- **Lumbar muscles and fascia:** the erector spinae, multifidus, quadratus lumborum, and thoracolumbar fascia — pain arises from sustained contraction, trigger points, fatigue, or reflex guarding in response to other lumbar pathologies. Multifidus atrophy and delayed activation are common motor-control findings in chronic NSLBP.
- **Ligaments:** the supraspinous, interspinous, and iliolumbar ligaments may be strained in acute flexion/rotation injuries.

> **Clinical Note:** The pathoanatomical source of NSLBP cannot be reliably identified in the majority of cases, and imaging findings (disc degeneration, facet arthropathy, mild disc bulges) correlate poorly with symptoms — they are equally common in asymptomatic individuals of the same age. This is the central paradox of NSLBP management: the pathology is real but the precise tissue driver is unknowable in most cases, so treatment targets function, movement, and pain modulation rather than tissue-specific repair. This is the single most important patient education point in this protocol.

### B8.2 Assessment Clues

Building on Module 1's standard assessment, with non-specific-LBP-specific additions:

| Finding | Typical Non-specific LBP pattern |
|---|---|
| Onset | Often insidious or related to a trivial mechanical trigger (e.g. "I bent over to pick something up" or "I slept awkwardly") rather than a high-force trauma — sudden onset with clear trauma should raise suspicion for fracture or disc herniation (B9/B12) |
| Location | Axial low back (between T12 and the gluteal folds), may refer to the buttock or posterior thigh, but does not radiate below the knee — this is the key discriminator from radiculopathy (B9). Pain may be central, paramedian, or unilateral |
| Pain behavior | Mechanical: provoked by sustained positions (prolonged sitting, standing, bending), specific movements (especially flexion, extension, or rotation), and relieved by changing position, walking, or lying down — this mechanical pattern is the defining feature and differentiates it from inflammatory (morning stiffness >1 hour, night pain) or systemic pathology |
| AROM | Restricted in one or more planes; pain-limited rather than weakness-limited; flexion, extension, and/or lateral flexion may be provocative; the most painful direction(s) guide treatment emphasis |
| PROM / passive segmental testing | Passive intervertebral motion (central and unilateral PA pressures) may reproduce familiar pain at specific levels or be diffusely restricted — but unlike B9/B10-B12, segmental findings are often non-specific and variable |
| Muscle testing | Lower extremity myotomes (L2-S1) are full and symmetrical — this is the key discriminator against radiculopathy (B9), where myotomal weakness is a hallmark. Hip and core musculature may be weak/deconditioned (not neurologically weak) contributing to the movement pattern |
| Neurological exam | Normal — no myotomal weakness, no dermatomal sensory loss, normal reflexes, negative straight leg raise (or mildly positive bilaterally, not unilateral radicular). This must be confirmed before committing to an NSLBP label (B8.3) |
| Special tests | SLR is negative (or only produces bilateral hamstring tightness, not radicular pain); slump test may be mildly positive bilaterally but not reproducing unilateral radicular symptoms; facet provocation tests (extension/rotation, unilateral PA pressures) may be positive; sacroiliac provocation tests may be positive (B11 if dominant) |
| Movement pattern | Observable movement dysfunctions — flexion-dominant pattern (pain provoked by flexion, relieved by extension), extension-dominant pattern (pain provoked by extension, relieved by flexion), or multidirectional pattern. This pattern guides the directional preference that drives exercise prescription (B8.7) |
| Psychosocial factors | Fear-avoidance beliefs, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and yellow flags (B8.12) are particularly relevant in NSLBP, more so than in many other musculoskeletal conditions — screen actively and address with education and graded exposure |

> **Clinical Note:** The single most important discriminator in this condition is a full neurological examination (Module 1.6). Any neurological deficit, any radiating pain below the knee, or a positive SLR reproducing radicular leg pain means you are not dealing with NSLBP — these patients belong to B9 (Sciatica/Disc Prolapse) or a neurological referral pathway, not this protocol. NSLBP is a diagnosis of exclusion, and the exclusion of radicular/neurological involvement is non-negotiable.

### B8.3 Differential Diagnosis

| Differential | Distinguishing feature against Non-specific LBP |
|---|---|
| Sciatica / Disc Prolapse (B9) | Positive SLR reproducing radicular leg pain; dermatomal sensory loss; myotomal weakness; reflex changes; pain radiates below the knee into the leg/foot |
| Facet Joint Syndrome (B10) | Pain reproduced/aggravated by extension and rotation; unilateral PA pressure at a specific level reproduces familiar pain; pain is more clearly segmental than diffuse NSLBP |
| SIJ Dysfunction (B11) | Positive SIJ provocation tests (≥3 of: thigh thrust, Gaenslen's, distraction, compression, sacral thrust); pain localized to the posterior pelvis, aggravated by sitting and rising |
| Lumbar Spondylosis/Spondylolisthesis (B12) | Typically older patients (>50) for spondylosis; younger patients for spondylolisthesis; X-ray shows degenerative changes, disc space narrowing, or listhesis; often a mixed mechanical/radicular presentation |
| Inflammatory Arthropathy (e.g. ankylosing spondylitis) | Marked morning stiffness (>1 hour, often >30 minutes), night pain unrelieved by position change, improvement with exercise, systemic symptoms, younger male patient, family history |
| Lumbar Myelopathy | Bilateral lower extremity paresthesia/weakness, gait disturbance, bowel/bladder involvement, UMN signs (hyperreflexia, clonus, positive Babinski) — these are red flags (B8.12) requiring immediate referral |
| Myofascial Pain | Pain is more regional than segmental; active trigger points in paraspinal muscles reproduce the patient's pain pattern on palpation; segmental mobility testing is relatively normal — this is often part of NSLBP, not a separate diagnosis, but treatment may emphasize dry needling/soft tissue work alongside exercise |
| Hip Pathology (e.g. OA, gluteal tendinopathy) | Pain localized to the groin, anterior thigh, or greater trochanter; hip ROM restriction (especially internal rotation), not reproduced by lumbar movements; FADIR/FABER tests positive |

### B8.4 Phase Goals (7-14-28 Day Framework)

| Phase | Typical window | Primary aim | Key metrics to track |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 — Acute/Protective | Day 1-7 | Reduce pain, reduce irritability, educate on staying active within pain limits (bed rest is actively discouraged), identify directional preference (which movements ease symptoms), establish activity modification | NPRS trend (rest and movement); ability to identify and modify provocative postures; identification of directional preference (flexion, extension, or sidelying); patient verbalizes "hurt does not equal harm" education |
| Phase 2 — Sub-acute/Restorative | Day 7-14 | Establish directional preference-based exercise, improve lumbar mobility and motor control, address postural dysfunction, begin core stabilization and graded activity | Reduced pain during movement; ability to perform directional preference exercise correctly; improved functional mobility; reduced fear-avoidance behaviors |
| Phase 3 — Remodeling/Functional | Day 14-28 | Build core and lumbopelvic endurance and strength, integrate into functional/occupational demands, return to full activity, address psychosocial barriers actively | Pain-free completion of previously provocative occupational/functional tasks; return to work/sport/activity at a meaningful level; patient demonstrates self-efficacy in managing symptoms; independent postural self-management |
| Phase 4 — Discharge/Maintenance | Beyond Day 28 (chronic/recurrent cases, or slow-responders) | Independent self-management, relapse-prevention education, ongoing conditioning, addressing yellow flags (B8.12) if present | Patient demonstrates independent home program; outcome measure (ODI or RMDQ) approaching normative score; patient has a clear, written flare-management plan |

> **Clinical Note:** The most important principle in NSLBP is that bed rest is actively harmful — patients should be advised to stay as active as possible within the limits of pain, modifying activities rather than ceasing them. Complete rest beyond the first 24-48 hours delays recovery, increases disability, and drives fear-avoidance behavior. This must be communicated explicitly and repeatedly.

### B8.5 Session Time-Block Application

| Phase | Opening electro (~10 min) | Manual therapy (~10-12 min) | Exercise (~15-20 min) | Closing electro (~10 min) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Heat pack (if comfort improves mobility and is not contraindicated by acute inflammation); TENS for pain modulation if high irritability; avoid heat if there is acute inflammation/tenderness (use ice in the first 24-48 hours post-acute injury) | Gentle soft tissue release to lumbar paraspinal and gluteal muscles; Grade I-II oscillations to symptomatic segments in pain-free or minimally symptomatic position; avoid aggressive mobilization in the first session if very irritable; identify directional preference | Directional preference exercise (e.g. press-ups for extension preference, knee-to-chest for flexion preference) performed within pain limits; gentle lumbar mobility exercises in the preferred direction; walking as tolerated; postural/ergonomic education (workstation setup, sitting posture, lifting technique) | Ice (if acute/inflammatory) or TENS (if irritability remains) |
| Phase 2 | Heat pack before manual therapy/exercise (to relax guarding and improve tissue extensibility) | Progress to Grade III-IV mobilization to restricted segments (unilateral and central PA pressures) based on findings; MET for lumbar/hip tightness; soft tissue release to trigger points; SIJ mobilization if indicated (B11 considerations) | Directional preference exercise progressed in volume/intensity; core stabilization (e.g. abdominal bracing, dead bug, bird-dog); lumbar mobility in the preferred direction; hip mobility work (to address associated hip tightness) | Minimal; optional ice if exercise provoked soreness |
| Phase 3 | Minimal/optional; shift to exercise-dominant sessions | Maintenance mobilization only for residual restrictions; hands-on time shifts to guided exercise form, functional training, and active coaching | Progressive core and lumbopelvic strengthening; functional and occupational endurance training; sport-specific retraining; graded return to provocative activities; postural endurance work | Minimal; taper in favor of exercise-dominant care |

> **Clinical Note:** Directional preference assessment (finding the movement direction that centralizes or eases symptoms) is the single most useful clinical tool in this condition. Ask: "Which movement makes your pain better?" and "Which movement makes it worse?" The patient's preferred direction — typically flexion (e.g. lying on the side in a fetal position) or extension (e.g. lying prone) — guides Phase 1 exercise prescription. If no clear directional preference exists, use a neutral spine/activity-pacing approach (B8.7).

### B8.6 Manual Therapy Detail

Manual therapy has moderate evidence in NSLBP, particularly in the acute phase to facilitate pain relief and improve mobility, but should always be paired with active exercise:

- **Phase 1:** Maitland Grade I-II oscillatory mobilizations — central PA pressures, unilateral PA pressures — applied to the symptomatic segment(s) identified in assessment (if any). Technique: oscillate at 2-3 Hz, low amplitude at the onset of resistance (not at end-range), pain-relief/irritability-reduction grade. Soft tissue release to lumbar paraspinal muscles, quadratus lumborum, gluteal muscles, and piriformis (palpate for trigger points/tension; apply ischemic compression for 30-60 seconds to active trigger points if not too irritable — if very irritable, avoid deep pressure and use gentle sustained pressure instead). Do not perform high-velocity thrust manipulation on the first visit until the patient's irritability and suitability is fully assessed.
- **Phase 2:** Progress to Grade III-IV mobilizations — larger amplitude oscillations reaching into the tissue resistance/restriction (end-range, but not pushing into sharp pain). Apply unilateral PA pressures at the segment(s) most restricted (reduced mobility on passive segmental testing). MET for lumbar rotation and side-flexion restriction: contract-relax with gentle pre-positioning into restriction, 5-second hold, 30-50% effort, repeat 3-5 times with increasing pre-positioning. SIJ mobilization if SIJ dysfunction is identified as a significant contributor (cross-reference B11 for detailed technique).
- **Phase 3:** Manual therapy tapers to maintenance and residual restriction targeting; the therapist's hands-on time increasingly shifts to guiding exercise form, particularly reinforcing correct core activation and movement quality during functional exercises.

> **Clinical Note on Lumbar Mobilization Technique:** The most commonly restricted segment in NSLBP is L4-5 or L5-S1. Unilateral PA pressures are generally more specific for facet-mediated pain and more discriminating than central PA pressures — a positive unilateral PA finding at a specific level (reproducing familiar pain) should be treated with targeted mobilization at that level in preference to a more global approach. If no segmental provocation is found, use a more general lumbar mobilization approach.

### B8.7 Exercise Prescription Detail

Exercise for NSLBP has four priority categories: (1) directional preference and mobility exercises, (2) core and lumbopelvic stabilization, (3) hip and lower limb mobility (given the high prevalence of hip tightness co-present), and (4) functional and occupational endurance training.

| Phase | Exercise focus | Example progressions |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Directional preference exercise in the pain-easing direction; gentle pain-free mobility; walking as tolerated; postural and ergonomic education | Extension preference: prone press-ups (McKenzie extension) — start with lying prone, progress to prone press-ups with hips down, progress to standing extension; perform in sets of 5-10, several times daily. Flexion preference: knee-to-chest in supine (single then double), supine pelvic tilt, sidelying fetal position for symptom relief; progress to seated flexion. No clear preference: gentle lumbar mobility in all planes within pain limits (cat-camel, pelvic tilts, gentle rotation), activity pacing education |
| Phase 2 | Progress directional preference exercise volume; core stabilization training; hip mobility; graded functional activity | Extension preference progression: increase press-up range/reps, add standing extension with hands on low back, add lumbar extension in quadruped. Flexion preference progression: progress from supine to standing forward flexion, add seated flexion, include hanging/lying traction. Core stabilization: dead bug (arms/legs), bird-dog, side-lying plank (regressed to modified plank if needed), abdominal bracing during activities. Hip mobility: kneeling hip flexor stretch, supine glute stretch, standing hamstring stretch |
| Phase 3 | Progressive core and lumbopelvic strengthening; functional/occupational endurance training; sport-specific retraining | Core strengthening progression: full plank, side plank, dead bug with resistance, bird-dog with resistance, anti-rotation drills (Pallof press). Functional training: squat/lunge/deadlift patterns with postural cueing, loaded carrying (farmer's carry), lifting technique practice (moderate loads with perfect form). Endurance training: graded walking/hiking program, cycling (if extension-tolerated). Sport-specific work: retraining running mechanics, golf swing, tennis serve, or other sport-specific postures with core stabilization cueing |

- **Dosage principle for NSLBP:** Start with low volume (e.g. 5-10 reps, 2-3 sets, 2-3 times daily for mobility; 3-5 sets of 8-15 reps for strengthening) and progress based on symptom response. Exercise should produce a tolerable flare (≤3/10 pain that settles within an hour) — this is the "sweet spot" for recovery. Complete avoidance of movement-based pain delays adaptation.
- **Pain-monitoring rule for this condition:** "Hurt does not equal harm" — exercise may produce a transient increase in symptoms (a "good" pain that settles quickly), but sharp or worsening pain that persists >1 hour is a signal to reduce load/volume. Distinguish between (a) muscle soreness/movement discomfort, and (b) sharp/aggravating pain — the former is acceptable and expected; the latter requires modification.
- **Walking prescription:** Walking is the single most accessible and effective early intervention for NSLBP. Prescribe: start with 5-10 minutes, 2-3 times daily, progressing by 5 minutes every few days as tolerated, aiming for 30-45 minutes daily. Walking must be pain-monitored: if pain increases during walking, reduce duration or pace; if pain settles during walking, it's a good sign.

### B8.8 Electrotherapy Detail

- **TENS:** Indicated for symptomatic pain relief in Phase 1, particularly for patients with high irritability or those who are not yet engaging effectively with active exercises due to pain. Conventional TENS (high-frequency, 80-120 Hz, low-intensity) applied to the lumbar paraspinal region — teach the patient home-use TENS if night pain or prolonged sitting pain is a barrier to sleep/work function.
- **Heat pack:** Useful before manual therapy and exercise in Phase 1-2 to relax guarding and improve tissue extensibility — a routine precursor to lumbar mobilization and stretching. Avoid if there is acute inflammation/swelling (first 24-48 hours post-acute injury).
- **Therapeutic ultrasound:** Weak evidence for NSLBP specifically — may be used as an adjunct for pain relief and muscle relaxation if the clinic includes it, but never substitutes for manual therapy or exercise time.
- **IFT:** Adjunctive for pain relief; following the same adjunctive principle as other conditions — not a substitute for active care.
- **Laser/LLLT:** Some evidence for short-term pain relief in NSLBP, but current evidence does not support it as a primary driver of recovery — use only as an adjunct where it is available and the patient responds symptomatically.

> **Clinical Note:** The evidence consistently supports exercise as the primary treatment for NSLBP, with manual therapy as a useful adjunct. Electrotherapy is a distant third-line intervention that should be used sparingly and never as a substitute for the active components of care (exercise and education).

### B8.9 Acupuncture / Dry Needling Detail

- Indicated for myofascial trigger points in the lumbar paraspinal muscles (erector spinae, multifidus, quadratus lumborum), gluteal muscles (gluteus medius, gluteus minimus), and piriformis, which are common in NSLBP and contribute significantly to the pain and guarding pattern.
- Particularly useful for trigger points in the quadratus lumborum (often palpated laterally in the lumbar region) and gluteus medius (a common source of referral pain to the lateral hip and buttock) — these are clinically common and highly responsive to needling.
- Rationale: reducing myofascial guarding can improve pain-free lumbar mobility, reduce referral symptoms, and allow more effective manual therapy and exercise in the same or following session — consistent with Module 2.5's acupuncture principle of enabling primary interventions.
- Not indicated as a primary treatment for lumbar joint dysfunction — it addresses the muscular component only, which is often secondary; if segmental mobility testing demonstrates a clear joint source, joint mobilization must also be performed; dry needling alone is insufficient.
- (Full needle selection, depth, technique, and safety precautions for each lumbar/gluteal muscle will be detailed in Module 6: Acupuncture/Dry Needling Protocol Library.)

### B8.10 Contraindications and Precautions

- **Lumbar manipulation (high-velocity thrust):** This is a moderate-risk intervention with potential for serious complications (disc herniation aggravation, cauda equina, vertebral fracture in osteoporosis). At Relife, lumbar manipulation is performed only by clinicians specifically trained and certified in spinal manipulation technique, and only after a full pre-manipulative safety screen (red flags, B8.12, and imaging where indicated). For non-certified clinicians, use Maitland mobilizations (Grade I-IV) as the primary manual therapy technique and do not substitute with thrust manipulation.
- **Acute radiculopathy (B9) red flags** — if a patient develops neurological symptoms (radiating leg pain, paresthesia, weakness) at any point during care, they should be reclassified to B9 and managed according to that protocol, not continued under the NSLBP label.
- **Cauda equina red flags** — saddle anesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, bilateral leg weakness, progressive neurological deficit — absolute contraindication to lumbar manual therapy; refer immediately for emergency assessment.
- **Osteoporosis** — use lower-grade mobilizations (Grade I-II) and avoid high-velocity thrust manipulation; patients with known osteoporosis and lumbar pain should be managed with exercise and low-intensity manual techniques only.
- **Vertebral fracture** — a history of significant trauma, prolonged steroid use, or osteoporosis with sudden-onset severe pain are red flags; imaging is warranted before manual therapy.
- **Inflammatory arthritis** (e.g. ankylosing spondylitis) — a relative contraindication to high-velocity lumbar manipulation; use only gentle, low-grade mobilization techniques and coordinate with the patient's rheumatologist if manipulation is considered.
- **No imaging for routine NSLBP** — the clinical guideline consensus is that imaging is not routinely indicated for NSLBP without red flags or failure to improve; avoid unnecessary imaging in the absence of clear indications.

### B8.11 Progression Criteria

**Progress to the next phase/loading level when:**
- Phase 1 → 2: NPRS at rest reduced to ≤3/10; directional preference identified; patient can correctly perform directional preference and basic mobility exercises without symptom reproduction; walking program established.
- Phase 2 → 3: Pain-free movement in the previously provocative direction improved; core stabilization exercises performed with good form; functional mobility improved (e.g. sitting >30 minutes without significant pain, standing >30 minutes without pain, bending to floor with minimal discomfort).
- Within Phase 3, progress load/complexity when current exercise load is tolerated with ≤3/10 exercise pain that settles within 1 hour, and no >24h flare.

**Regress or hold when:**
- Pain during exercise exceeds 3/10 or persists >1 hour post-exercise; or a >24h flare occurs (return to the prior tolerated load level).
- New neurological symptoms appear (radiating leg pain, paresthesia, weakness) — halt lumbar mobilization and re-classify toward B9/neurological referral immediately.
- Lumbar ROM worsens on reassessment — screen for a new aggravating factor or alternate diagnosis before continuing.
- Pain is centralizing toward the lower back (a good sign — reducing volume but not discontinuing).
- Pain is peripheralizing toward the leg (a bad sign — stop the provoking exercise and reassess).

### B8.12 Red Flags Specific to This Condition

In addition to the universal red flags (Module 1, 1.2):

| Red Flag Category | Specific Indicators | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Cauda Equina Syndrome | Saddle anesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, bilateral leg weakness, progressive neurological deficit, perianal sensory loss | Immediate emergency referral — do not perform lumbar manual therapy |
| Vertebral Fracture | Significant trauma (MVC, fall from height), prolonged steroid use, age >70 with minor trauma, sudden severe pain, inability to weight-bear | Urgent imaging and orthopedic referral |
| Malignancy/Infection | Unexplained weight loss, history of malignancy, fever/chills, recent infection, night pain unrelieved by position, immunosuppression, IV drug use | Urgent medical referral for blood work and imaging |
| Inflammatory Arthropathy | Marked morning stiffness (>1 hour), night pain, improvement with exercise, multiple joint involvement, systemic symptoms (fatigue, fever, weight loss), elevated ESR/CRP | Rheumatology referral; avoid aggressive manual therapy until diagnosis clarified |
| Progressive Neurological Deficit | New or worsening weakness, sensory loss, or reflex changes — especially if bilateral or affecting bowel/bladder | Urgent neurological/orthopedic referral; do not continue conservative care |
| Cauda Equina-equivalent (Lumbar Myelopathy) | Bilateral lower extremity paresthesia/weakness, gait disturbance, bowel/bladder involvement, UMN signs (hyperreflexia, clonus, positive Babinski) | Immediate neurological referral; do not perform lumbar manual therapy |

**Yellow Flags (Psychosocial risk factors for chronicity)** — particularly important in NSLBP:

- Fear-avoidance beliefs: "Movement will damage my back", "I must not bend or lift"
- Catastrophizing: "My back is ruined", "I'll never get better"
- Low mood/depression
- Passive coping strategies: relying on passive modalities rather than active self-management
- Work-related factors: job dissatisfaction, manual work, lack of workplace accommodations
- Litigation/compensation claims
- Social support deficit

> **Clinical Note:** Yellow flags predict poor outcome more strongly than the severity of physical findings in NSLBP. Address them explicitly through education (challenging fear-avoidance beliefs), graded exposure (demonstrating that movement is safe), and cognitive-behavioral principles (reframing "hurt" as not necessarily "harm"). If yellow flags are prominent, consider a psychologically-informed physiotherapy approach or referral to a clinical psychologist.

### B8.13 Home Exercise Program

| Phase | Home program emphasis | Example content |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Directional preference exercise; gentle pain-free movement; walking as tolerated; postural/ergonomic self-correction | Extension preference: prone press-ups 5-10 reps, 3-5 times daily; standing extension; avoid prolonged sitting. Flexion preference: knee-to-chest 5-10 reps, 3-5 times daily; supine pelvic tilt; avoid prolonged standing. No clear preference: gentle cat-camel, pelvic tilts, walking; avoid sustained positions. Ergonomic/workstation check; "take a break every 30 minutes" guidance; activity pacing |
| Phase 2 | Structured daily directional preference program; core stabilization; hip mobility; graded functional activity | Extension preference progression: press-ups with increased range/reps, standing extension. Flexion preference progression: standing forward flexion, seated flexion. Core stabilization: dead bug, bird-dog, side-lying plank (modified), abdominal bracing during daily activities. Hip mobility: kneeling hip flexor stretch, supine glute stretch, standing hamstring stretch. Walking progression: increase by 5 minutes every few days |
| Phase 3 | Progressive home strengthening program; functional and occupational retraining; long-term relapse prevention | Core progression: full plank, side plank, Pallof press (if band available). Functional training: squat/lunge/deadlift patterns with form cueing, loaded carrying. Endurance training: graded walking/hiking program. Sport-specific drills: running mechanics, golf swing, tennis serve with core cueing |

- **Most important patient education message for this condition:** "Hurt does not mean harm. Your back is strong. Moving, even when it hurts a little, will help you recover faster than resting. The worst thing you can do is stop moving completely." Frame this explicitly and repeatedly.
- **Flare management plan:** provide every patient with a written plan for managing future flares: (1) stay active and continue walking; (2) resume directional preference exercises; (3) avoid prolonged sitting or standing; (4) modify lifting technique; (5) use heat/ice for comfort; (6) if symptoms persist >48 hours or new neurological symptoms appear, contact the clinic.
- Address workplace ergonomics explicitly: desk setup, chair adjustment, monitor height, lumbar support, regular breaks, lifting technique. Many NSLBP patients are desk workers; an optimized workstation is a key relapse prevention strategy.
- Address sleep posture: sidelying with a pillow between knees is generally best for the lumbar spine; supine with a pillow under the knees is also good; avoid prolonged prone lying.
- Address lifting technique: teach "sit-to-stand" lifting (hinge at the hips, keep the back straight, engage the core, keep the load close to the body) — practice it with light loads in the clinic so the patient can do it safely at home.
- (Full templated home program handouts will be built in Module 10: Home Exercise Program Templates.)

### B8.14 Evidence Grade Summary

| Intervention | Evidence Level | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Exercise therapy (any form) | High | Strongest evidence for NSLBP — exercise is the primary treatment; type of exercise matters less than adherence and active participation |
| Manual therapy (mobilization, manipulation) + exercise combined | Moderate-High | Combined approach more effective than exercise alone; manual therapy alone is less effective without exercise |
| Education and advice (staying active, "hurt ≠ harm") | Moderate-High | Strong evidence for addressing fear-avoidance; essential component of care |
| Walking program | Moderate | Direct evidence; highly accessible and effective for most patients |
| Cognitive-behavioral / psychologically-informed approaches | Moderate-High | For patients with prominent yellow flags; may be essential for some patients |
| TENS | Low-Moderate | Symptomatic adjunct for pain modulation, especially for sleep disruption |
| Heat therapy | Low-Moderate | Symptomatic/comfort only; useful for muscle relaxation and comfort |
| Therapeutic ultrasound / IFT / laser | Low | Weak adjunctive evidence; never a substitute for exercise or education |
| Lumbar traction | Low (for this condition specifically) | Weak evidence for NSLBP; unlike B9 (radiculopathy), traction is not a standard component here |
| Dry needling (secondary myofascial guarding) | Low-Moderate | Adjunct only; addresses muscular component, not the primary NSLBP driver |
| Bed rest | Harmful | Active rest (staying active within pain limits) is recommended; bed rest beyond 1-2 days delays recovery |

### B8.15 Outcome Measures & Cutoffs

| Measure | What it captures | Clinically meaningful change (MCID) | When to administer |
|---|---|---|---|
| ODI (Oswestry Disability Index) | LBP-specific disability | ~10-12 points | Baseline, then every reassessment point |
| RMDQ (Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire) | LBP-specific disability | ~3-5 points | Baseline, then every reassessment point |
| PSFS (Patient-Specific Functional Scale) | Patient-defined functional goals | ~2 points | Baseline, then every reassessment point |
| NPRS (rest and movement) | Pain intensity | ~2 points | Every session |
| STarT Back Screening Tool | Risk stratification for chronicity | Determines treatment intensity (low/medium/high), not a change-score measure | Baseline — strongly recommended for all NSLBP patients |

> **Clinical Note:** The STarT Back Screening Tool is a 9-item questionnaire that stratifies patients into low, medium, or high risk for persistent disability. It should be administered at baseline for all NSLBP patients. Low-risk patients can be managed with a simple directional-preference exercise program and education; medium-risk patients benefit from a more structured exercise and manual therapy program; high-risk patients require psychologically-informed physiotherapy (addressing yellow flags) and may benefit from a multidisciplinary approach.

### B8.16 AI Condition Card (RAG Metadata)

- **Condition:** Non-specific Low Back Pain (NSLBP)
- **Framework:** Standard 7-14-28 day phase framework; directional preference guides exercise prescription; yellow flags are actively screened
- **Key Symptoms:** axial lumbar pain, mechanical pattern (movement/posture-provoked, relieved by position change), normal neurological exam, no radiating pain below the knee
- **Red Flags:** cauda equina (saddle anesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction), fracture (significant trauma, osteoporosis, steroid use), malignancy/infection (weight loss, fever, night pain, history of malignancy), progressive neurological deficit, inflammatory arthropathy (prolonged morning stiffness >1 hour), myelopathy (bilateral/UMN signs)
- **Yellow Flags (psychosocial):** fear-avoidance beliefs, catastrophizing, low mood, passive coping, work dissatisfaction, litigation
- **Primary Treatment:** exercise therapy (directional preference, core stabilization, functional training), education and advice (staying active, "hurt ≠ harm"), manual therapy as an adjunct
