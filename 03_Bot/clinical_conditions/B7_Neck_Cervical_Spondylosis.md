## B7. Neck: Cervical Spondylosis

> **Format note:** This protocol applies the standard Module 2.3 7-14-28 day phase framework to cervical spondylosis — the degenerative disc and facet joint disease of the cervical spine that underlies much of the age-related mechanical and radicular cervical pathology seen in outpatient practice. Spondylosis is not a single symptom pattern but a radiographic/structural process (disc height loss, osteophyte formation, facet hypertrophy) that produces a mixed clinical picture overlapping with both B5 (Mechanical Neck Pain) and B6 (Cervical Radiculopathy) — this section focuses on what is specific to the degenerative, progressive, and typically older-patient nature of the condition, and should be read alongside B5/B6 for the symptom-management detail that carries over directly.

### B7.1 Anatomy Link

Cervical spondylosis is the cumulative structural consequence of age-related disc and facet joint degeneration, most pronounced at the most mobile subaxial segments (C5-6 and C6-7).

- **Disc degeneration:** loss of disc hydration and height (the disc's nucleus pulposus loses proteoglycan content with age), reducing its shock-absorbing capacity and increasing load transfer to the facet joints and uncovertebral joints — this is the initiating structural change in most cases.
- **Osteophyte formation:** in response to altered loading, osteophytes develop at the vertebral body margins (anterior and posterior) and at the uncovertebral joints — posterior and uncovertebral osteophytes are the ones clinically relevant to foraminal and central canal narrowing.
- **Facet joint hypertrophy:** the facet joints undergo osteoarthritic change (cartilage loss, capsular thickening, osteophyte formation) as they bear increased load from disc height loss — this contributes to both mechanical pain (facet-mediated, as in B5) and further foraminal narrowing.
- **Ligamentum flavum thickening/buckling:** contributes to central canal narrowing, particularly relevant when myelopathy risk is being screened (B7.12).
- **Clinical spectrum:** spondylosis can be radiographically present and entirely asymptomatic (a very common incidental finding, especially beyond age 50), can present as axial mechanical neck pain (facet/disc-mediated, overlapping with B5), as radiculopathy from foraminal narrowing (overlapping with B6), or — least commonly but most seriously — as myelopathy from central canal stenosis (B7.12). Distinguishing which of these clinical pictures is dominant in a given patient is the central reasoning task of this protocol.

> **Clinical Note:** Because spondylotic changes are near-universal on imaging beyond middle age, imaging findings alone should never be assumed to explain a patient's symptoms — always correlate radiographic severity with the clinical exam (B7.2) before attributing a symptom pattern to spondylosis, and be cautious about over-medicalizing an incidental imaging finding when a patient's presentation is otherwise consistent with straightforward mechanical neck pain (B5).

### B7.2 Assessment Clues

Building on Module 1's standard assessment and the findings detailed in B5 and B6, with spondylosis-specific additions:

| Finding | Typical Cervical Spondylosis pattern |
|---|---|
| Age/demographic | Typically >50; radiographic changes increase steadily with age and are near-universal by the 60s-70s even in asymptomatic individuals |
| Onset | Gradual, insidious, often with a long history of intermittent neck pain/stiffness rather than a single triggering event |
| Symptom pattern | Mixed — axial mechanical pain (as B5), radicular arm symptoms (as B6), or both concurrently; symptoms often fluctuate with activity/posture over a background of chronic low-grade stiffness |
| AROM | Global restriction across multiple planes (not just extension/rotation as in pure mechanical neck pain), often with palpable/audible crepitus on movement, reflecting multi-level structural change rather than a single-segment mechanical driver |
| Morning stiffness | Common and often more pronounced than in B5, reflecting the degenerative/osteoarthritic component, though it should still resolve within an hour — longer morning stiffness raises suspicion for an inflammatory arthropathy and should prompt reconsideration of the working diagnosis |
| Neurological exam | May be entirely normal (axial-pattern spondylosis), show radicular signs (B6.2 pattern, if foraminal narrowing is symptomatic), or show myelopathic signs (B7.12) — this exam result determines which sub-pathway of this protocol applies |
| Imaging correlation | X-ray showing disc space narrowing, osteophytes, facet arthropathy; MRI (where progression or radicular/myelopathic signs warrant it) showing foraminal or central canal narrowing correlating with the clinical picture |

> **Clinical Note:** The neurological exam — not the imaging report — is what determines the treatment pathway. A patient with extensive radiographic spondylosis but a normal neurological exam and axial-pattern pain is managed essentially as B5 with spondylosis-specific pacing and expectation-setting (B7.4); a patient with radicular signs is managed essentially as B6 with spondylosis-specific chronicity considerations; a patient with myelopathic signs is an urgent referral (B7.12), regardless of how mild the axial pain seems.

### B7.3 Differential Diagnosis

| Differential | Distinguishing feature against Cervical Spondylosis |
|---|---|
| Mechanical Neck Pain (B5) in a younger patient | Younger age, no significant degenerative imaging findings if obtained, single-segment rather than multi-level restriction pattern |
| Cervical Radiculopathy, disc-herniation-dominant (B6) | Younger patient, acute onset, single-level soft disc herniation on imaging rather than a chronic multi-level degenerative picture — though spondylotic foraminal narrowing is itself a common cause of radiculopathy in older patients, making B6 and B7 frequently co-diagnosed rather than mutually exclusive |
| Cervical Myelopathy | Bilateral limb involvement, gait disturbance, hand clumsiness/fine motor loss, UMN signs — this is a distinct, urgent clinical entity that can arise from severe spondylotic canal narrowing (cervical spondylotic myelopathy is in fact the most common cause of spinal cord dysfunction in older adults) and must be actively screened for at every visit (B7.12) |
| Inflammatory arthropathy (rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis) | Prolonged morning stiffness (>1 hour), systemic/multi-joint involvement, elevated inflammatory markers, upper cervical instability concerns (particularly atlantoaxial in RA) — changes manual therapy safety considerations significantly (B7.10) |
| Polymyalgia rheumatica / other systemic causes of neck and shoulder girdle pain | Systemic symptoms, marked morning stiffness, elevated ESR/CRP, rapid response to corticosteroids — a medical rather than physiotherapy-primary diagnosis |

### B7.4 Phase Goals (7-14-28 Day Framework)

| Phase | Typical window | Primary aim | Key metrics to track |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 — Acute/Protective | Day 1-7 | Manage acute flare-up pain against a background of chronic degenerative change; identify whether the current presentation is axial, radicular, or mixed; screen thoroughly for myelopathy | NPRS trend; neurological exam at baseline and confirmed stable; clarity on which sub-pathway (B5-pattern, B6-pattern, or mixed) applies |
| Phase 2 — Sub-acute/Restorative | Day 7-14 | Progress mobility and strength per the applicable sub-pathway (B5 or B6 principles), with pacing adjusted for chronicity and, where present, comorbid degenerative stiffness | Functional ROM gains relative to this patient's own baseline (not necessarily age-matched norms, since some restriction may be structurally fixed); symptom trend |
| Phase 3 — Remodeling/Functional | Day 14-28 | Build durable strength and endurance; establish a long-term self-management plan appropriate for a chronic, progressive-but-manageable structural condition | Functional task tolerance; patient's understanding of the chronic-but-manageable nature of the condition |
| Phase 4 — Discharge/Maintenance | Beyond Day 28 | Long-term conditioning and monitoring, given the progressive (though usually slowly progressive) structural nature of the underlying pathology | Independent long-term maintenance program; scheduled monitoring for new neurological symptoms given ongoing degenerative risk |

> **Clinical Note:** Unlike B5 (where a full return to symptom-free baseline is a reasonable target) and B6 (where most cases substantially resolve), cervical spondylosis is a chronic structural condition — the realistic goal is durable symptom control and functional capacity, not structural reversal or permanent resolution of the underlying degeneration. Set this expectation explicitly with the patient at the outset (B7.13).

### B7.5 Session Time-Block Application

| Phase | Opening electro (~10 min) | Manual therapy (~10-12 min) | Exercise (~15-20 min) | Closing electro (~10 min) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Hot pack for stiffness/guarding; TENS if irritability is high | Gentle Grade I-II mobilization to non-provocative segments; if radicular signs are present, follow B6.6's provocative-direction precautions rather than B5.6's more permissive approach | Gentle multi-plane AROM within tolerance; positioning education; if radicular signs present, follow B6.7 principles | Ice or TENS depending on irritability |
| Phase 2 | Hot pack before mobilization/exercise to address the degenerative stiffness component | Progress mobilization per the applicable sub-pathway (B5.6 for axial-pattern, B6.6 for radicular-pattern), typically with a more conservative progression rate than a younger single-segment presentation given multi-level involvement | Progressive multi-plane ROM and strengthening per sub-pathway; deep neck flexor and cervicoscapular work as tolerated | Minimal; optional ice |
| Phase 3 | Minimal/optional | Maintenance mobilization; hands-on time shifts to exercise form and long-term self-management coaching | Progressive strengthening and endurance building; explicit transition toward an independent long-term maintenance program | Minimal; taper in favor of exercise-dominant care |

### B7.6 Manual Therapy Detail

- **General principle:** manual therapy technique selection follows whichever sub-pathway (B5-pattern axial or B6-pattern radicular) matches the patient's clinical presentation at that point in time — this section adds spondylosis-specific modifications rather than a separate technique set.
- **Grade and progression rate:** given multi-level structural change and typically older patient age, favor a more gradual progression through mobilization grades than in a younger single-segment mechanical neck pain patient, and reassess response more frequently before advancing.
- **Pre-manipulative safety screening (B5.10) is non-negotiable** and arguably more important in this population — osteophytic and degenerative change can alter normal vertebral artery anatomy and course, and older age itself is a relevant risk consideration for manual therapy safety screening.
- **Facet-predominant presentations:** where segmental mobility testing (B5.1) identifies a clear facet-mediated pain source at one or two levels despite the broader degenerative background, targeted unilateral PA mobilization to those specific levels (as in B5.6) remains appropriate and useful.
- **Avoid aggressive end-range extension mobilization** in patients with confirmed significant foraminal or central canal narrowing on imaging, even in the absence of current radicular/myelopathic signs, since extension further narrows an already-compromised space.

### B7.7 Exercise Prescription Detail

| Phase | Exercise focus | Example progressions |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Gentle multi-plane AROM within tolerance; positioning/pacing education for flare management | Pain-free active ROM in all planes, gentle chin-tucks, activity pacing education specific to managing a chronic condition with periodic flares |
| Phase 2 | Deep neck flexor endurance (as B5.7) if axial-pattern dominant; neurodynamic mobility (as B6.7) if radicular signs present; general mobility maintenance across all planes | Craniocervical flexion progression; nerve slider progression if applicable; gentle rotation/side-flexion mobility work respecting any structurally-narrowed levels |
| Phase 3 | Progressive cervicoscapular and postural strengthening; durable long-term conditioning program | Resistance-band cervicoscapular strengthening; postural endurance training; an explicit, written long-term maintenance program the patient can sustain independently |

- **Dosage principle specific to this condition:** because the underlying structural change is not reversible, exercise dosing prioritizes sustainable, ongoing symptom control and functional capacity over an aggressive short-course loading progression — pace advancement more conservatively than in B5/B6's typical single-episode presentation, and plan explicitly for a longer-term (beyond Day 28) maintenance relationship with the exercise program.
- **Multi-plane mobility work** (not just the extension/rotation emphasis of B5) is relevant here because spondylotic restriction is typically more global across planes than the single-direction pattern of straightforward mechanical neck pain.

### B7.8 Electrotherapy Detail

Follows the same general adjunctive principles as B5.8/B6.8 — hot pack before mobility work for the degenerative stiffness component, TENS for symptomatic relief, weak/adjunctive evidence for ultrasound/IFT/laser. No electrotherapy modality alters the underlying degenerative structural process; all are used for comfort and to enable more effective manual therapy and exercise participation.

### B7.9 Acupuncture / Dry Needling Detail

Same rationale and indication as B5.9/B6.9 — addresses secondary myofascial guarding in the cervical and periscapular musculature that commonly develops as a compensatory response to chronic degenerative stiffness and pain. Not indicated as a treatment for the structural degenerative process itself. (Full needle selection, depth, technique, and safety precautions will be detailed in Module 6.)

### B7.10 Contraindications and Precautions

- **Cervical manipulation:** same certification and pre-manipulative safety screen requirements as B5.10/B6.10 apply, with heightened caution given the typically older patient population and potential for altered vertebral artery anatomy from degenerative change.
- **Inflammatory arthropathy comorbidity:** if rheumatoid arthritis or another inflammatory arthropathy is present or suspected, screen specifically for atlantoaxial instability (a recognized RA complication) before any cervical manual therapy — this is a distinct and more urgent precaution than routine spondylosis management and may contraindicate manual therapy pending medical clearance.
- **Osteoporosis:** common in this age group — use lower-grade mobilization techniques and avoid high-velocity thrust manipulation, following the same principle as B5.10.
- **New or progressive neurological symptoms at any point:** reclassify toward B6 (radiculopathy) or urgent myelopathy referral (B7.12) immediately; do not continue routine spondylosis management under an unchanged plan.
- **Realistic goal-setting:** avoid implying to the patient that physiotherapy will reverse or eliminate the structural degenerative changes — overpromising structural correction undermines trust and sets up unrealistic expectations for a chronic condition.

### B7.11 Progression Criteria

**Progress to the next phase/loading level when:**
- Phase 1 → 2: The current flare's pain has settled to a manageable baseline and the applicable sub-pathway (axial vs. radicular) has been clearly established with a stable neurological exam.
- Phase 2 → 3: Functional ROM and strength gains are trending upward relative to this patient's own baseline; symptom control is sufficient to tolerate progressive loading.
- Within Phase 3, progress load/complexity gradually, with more frequent reassessment checkpoints than in a younger single-level presentation, given the higher likelihood of a slower and less linear recovery trajectory.

**Regress or hold when:**
- A flare significantly exceeds the patient's typical pattern or fails to settle with the usual self-management strategies — reassess for a new acute-on-chronic driver rather than assuming it is simply "more of the same."
- Any new neurological symptom appears — halt progression and reclassify per B7.3/B7.12 immediately.
- The patient shows reduced tolerance for previously-comfortable loading, which in a degenerative condition may reflect genuine slow structural progression rather than a simple deconditioning or flare pattern — flag for physician review if this trend persists.

### B7.12 Red Flags Specific to This Condition

In addition to the universal red flags (Module 1, 1.2), B5.12, and B6.12:

- **Cervical spondylotic myelopathy** — the most important condition-specific red flag category here, given that cervical spondylosis is the leading cause of spinal cord dysfunction in older adults. Screen actively at every visit for: bilateral upper extremity paresthesia or clumsiness (dropping objects, difficulty with fine motor tasks like buttons), gait disturbance or unsteadiness, bowel/bladder changes, hyperreflexia, clonus, positive Hoffman's or Babinski signs, and broad-based or spastic gait. Any of these findings warrants immediate referral for imaging and specialist assessment — do not continue routine conservative management.
- **Insidious, slowly progressive myelopathic signs** are easy to miss because the condition is chronic and the patient may attribute early changes (mild clumsiness, occasional stumbling) to normal aging rather than reporting them proactively — actively ask about these symptoms rather than waiting for spontaneous report.
- **Rapid change in a previously stable, chronic presentation** — a sudden worsening or a new symptom pattern in a patient with long-standing stable spondylosis should not be assumed to be "just a flare" without re-screening for red flags.

### B7.13 Home Exercise Program

| Phase | Home program emphasis | Example content |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Gentle multi-plane mobility maintenance; flare-management pacing education | Pain-free active ROM in all planes several times daily, activity pacing strategies for managing a chronic condition with periodic flares |
| Phase 2 | Structured program following the applicable sub-pathway (deep neck flexor endurance if axial, neurodynamic mobility if radicular) | Per B5.13 or B6.13 as applicable, adjusted for a more gradual progression rate |
| Phase 3 | Durable, sustainable long-term maintenance program the patient can continue indefinitely | Resistance-band cervicoscapular strengthening, postural endurance work, an explicit written long-term (beyond-discharge) program with realistic expectations about the chronic nature of the condition |

- Educate the patient explicitly that cervical spondylosis is a chronic, typically slowly progressive structural condition — the home program is a permanent, ongoing conditioning tool rather than a course that "cures" the underlying degeneration, similar in framing to B5's postural-syndrome patients but with an added emphasis on monitoring for new neurological symptoms over time given the myelopathy risk profile (B7.12).
- Provide clear, written guidance on which new symptoms warrant contacting the clinic promptly (any new arm weakness/numbness, hand clumsiness, gait change, bowel/bladder change) rather than waiting for the next scheduled visit.

### B7.15 Outcome Measures & Cutoffs

| Measure | What it captures | Clinically meaningful change (MCID) | When to administer |
|---|---|---|---|
| NDI (Neck Disability Index) | Neck-specific pain and disability | ~5 points (or ~19% of scale) | Baseline, then every reassessment point |
| NPRS | Pain intensity | ~2 points | Every session |
| mJOA (modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association) score, or equivalent myelopathy screening measure | Myelopathy severity, where signs are present or suspected | Any decline is clinically significant — this measure is for monitoring/referral urgency, not routine physiotherapy progression tracking | At intake if myelopathic signs present, and at any point new signs emerge |
