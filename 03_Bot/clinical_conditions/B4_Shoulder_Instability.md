## B4. Shoulder: Instability

> **Format note:** Shoulder instability is a distinct clinical entity from B1-B3 — it is fundamentally a problem of excessive or uncontrolled glenohumeral translation (structural, functional, or both), not primarily a tendon-loading or capsular-restriction problem. The exercise emphasis here (dynamic stability, proprioception, controlled range) differs meaningfully from B2/B3, even though some overlap exists where instability has caused secondary impingement or cuff overload. This protocol follows the standard 7-14-28 framework for atraumatic/subtle instability; frank traumatic dislocation has its own accelerated early-phase timeline noted in B4.4.

### B4.1 Anatomy Link

Glenohumeral stability depends on both static restraints (the glenoid labrum, glenohumeral ligaments, joint capsule, negative intra-articular pressure) and dynamic restraints (the rotator cuff and scapular musculature, which centre the humeral head via concavity-compression and fine-tune joint position throughout range).

- The glenoid is a shallow socket relative to the humeral head — the labrum deepens it by roughly 50% and provides the attachment for the glenohumeral ligaments; a labral tear (e.g. a **Bankart lesion**, anteroinferior labral detachment, typically from a traumatic anterior dislocation) removes both the static restraint and the ligament anchor, predisposing to recurrent instability.
- The inferior glenohumeral ligament complex is the primary static restraint against anterior-inferior translation in the abducted, externally rotated position (the position of greatest vulnerability, e.g. throwing/overhead cocking phase) — this is why apprehension testing is performed in this specific position (B4.2).
- **Traumatic instability** (a specific dislocation/subluxation event, usually anterior, from a fall or forced external rotation/abduction) commonly involves structural lesions — Bankart lesion, Hill-Sachs lesion (a compression fracture of the posterolateral humeral head from impaction against the glenoid rim during dislocation) — and carries a materially higher recurrence risk in younger patients.
- **Atraumatic/multidirectional instability (MDI)** typically arises from underlying generalized ligamentous laxity (often bilateral, sometimes with a positive Beighton hypermobility score) combined with inadequate dynamic muscular control, rather than a discrete structural lesion — this population is generally more physiotherapy-responsive than a structural Bankart/Hill-Sachs presentation and should be identified explicitly, since it drives a different management emphasis (B4.4-B4.7).
- The classic **TUBS** (Traumatic, Unidirectional, Bankart lesion, Surgery often required) versus **AMBRI** (Atraumatic, Multidirectional, Bilateral, Rehabilitation first-line, Inferior capsular shift only if surgery needed) framework is a useful clinical-reasoning shorthand for this distinction, though most real presentations fall somewhere on a spectrum between these two poles rather than cleanly at either end.

### B4.2 Assessment Clues

Building on Module 1's standard assessment, with instability-specific additions:

| Finding | Typical Instability pattern |
|---|---|
| History | A specific traumatic dislocation/subluxation event (TUBS-leaning) vs. insidious onset of a sense of looseness/apprehension without a clear traumatic event, often with a history of similar issues in other joints (AMBRI-leaning) |
| Apprehension test | Positive: apprehension (not just pain) reproduced with the arm in 90° abduction, external rotation, and an anteriorly-directed force — a hallmark test for anterior instability |
| Relocation test (Jobe) | Apprehension/pain reduces when a posteriorly-directed force is applied during the apprehension position — supports an anterior instability diagnosis and helps distinguish instability-related pain from primary impingement |
| Sulcus sign | A visible/palpable inferior sulcus below the acromion with inferior traction on an adducted arm — indicates inferior laxity, more associated with MDI |
| Generalized joint laxity | Beighton hypermobility score, contralateral shoulder laxity, family history of "loose joints" — supports an AMBRI/MDI classification |
| Load and Shift test | Graded assessment of anterior/posterior humeral head translation on the glenoid compared to the contralateral side — quantifies the degree of translation, not just symptom reproduction |
| Scapular control | Scapular dyskinesis is common and often secondary to (or compounding) the instability — assess as in B3.2 |
| Functional pattern | Symptoms/apprehension characteristically reproduced in the specific provocative position relevant to the patient's history or sport (e.g. cocking phase of throwing, bench press bottom position, swimming catch phase) |

> **Clinical Note:** True apprehension (fear of the shoulder "coming out," often with a facial/verbal apprehension response) is a more specific finding than pain alone during the apprehension test — patients with primary impingement or tendinopathy can report pain in a similar test position without true apprehension; document which response was actually observed, since this materially changes the working diagnosis.

### B4.3 Differential Diagnosis

| Differential | Distinguishing feature against Instability |
|---|---|
| Subacromial Impingement (B3) | Positive Neer's/Hawkins-Kennedy without true apprehension; painful arc pattern; no sense of the joint "giving way" or dislocating |
| Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy/Tear (B2) | Resisted weakness/pain as the dominant finding; no apprehension or translation abnormality on load-and-shift testing |
| Frozen Shoulder (B1) | Global capsular restriction rather than excessive translation; instability testing is not applicable in a genuinely frozen, stiff joint |
| SLAP Lesion (Superior Labrum Anterior-Posterior tear) | Can coexist with instability (especially traumatic); active compression test (O'Brien's) and pain deep in the joint with overhead activity are more specific to SLAP; often requires imaging to differentiate definitively from pure instability |
| Generalized Joint Hypermobility Syndrome/Ehlers-Danlos spectrum | Instability is one manifestation of a wider multi-joint hypermobility pattern; screen with Beighton score and other joint involvement — management still follows the AMBRI/MDI approach but with attention to whole-body conditioning, not just the shoulder |

### B4.4 Phase Goals (7-14-28 Day Framework, with Traumatic-Dislocation Modification)

| Phase | Typical window | Primary aim | Key metrics to track |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 — Acute/Protective | Day 1-7 (post-injury) or Day 1-7 (start of conservative program for atraumatic presentations) | For a first-time traumatic dislocation: brief period of relative protection (sling comfort-based, not prolonged rigid immobilization — evidence does not support extended immobilization for reducing recurrence in most age groups), pain control, gentle pain-free ROM initiation once acutely settled. For atraumatic/AMBRI: begin scapular and rotator cuff activation immediately, since there is no acute structural injury to protect | Pain-free passive/active-assisted ROM within a protected arc (avoiding the position of instability, e.g. combined abduction-external rotation for anterior instability); patient understands which positions to avoid |
| Phase 2 — Sub-acute/Restorative | Day 7-14 | Restore full pain-free ROM, begin dynamic stability/proprioceptive training and rotator cuff strengthening in progressively less protected positions | Improved scapular control; rotator cuff strength trending toward symmetry; tolerance of mid-range loaded positions without apprehension |
| Phase 3 — Remodeling/Functional | Day 14-28 | Progressive strengthening into the previously provocative/vulnerable position (e.g. abduction-external rotation), sport/occupation-specific dynamic stability training, graded return-to-activity | Absence of apprehension in the previously provocative position under load; functional/sport-specific task completion matching intake goals (Module 1.1) |
| Phase 4 — Discharge/Maintenance | Beyond Day 28 (most instability cases, especially recurrent or MDI, benefit from an extended maintenance phase) | Long-term dynamic stability maintenance program, particularly for overhead athletes or those with generalized laxity, given the elevated recurrence risk in this population | Independent, sustained maintenance program; documented recurrence-risk education |

- **Recurrence risk is age- and mechanism-dependent** and should be discussed explicitly with the patient and, where a first-time traumatic anterior dislocation occurs in a young (<25), high-demand or contact-sport patient, flagged to the referring physician as a population where early surgical stabilization (rather than prolonged conservative management) has evidence for reducing recurrence — this is a shared-decision point, not a physiotherapy-only decision (B4.10).
- For AMBRI/MDI presentations, expect a longer overall conditioning timeline than the 28-day window suggests for full resolution of the underlying laxity-related instability — set the discharge target at demonstrated independent dynamic control, not elimination of joint laxity itself (laxity is a constitutional trait, not a pathology to be "fixed").

### B4.5 Session Time-Block Application

| Phase | Opening electro (~10 min) | Manual therapy (~10-12 min) | Exercise (~15-20 min) | Closing electro (~10 min) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Hot pack for comfort | Gentle soft tissue work to guarding periscapular muscles; avoid any stretching or mobilization technique that pushes toward the direction of instability (e.g. aggressive posterior-capsule stretching in an anteriorly-unstable shoulder can worsen the underlying laxity) | Pain-free active-assisted ROM within a protected arc; scapular setting; rotator cuff isometrics in safe, mid-range positions | Ice/TENS if needed |
| Phase 2 | Hot pack optional | Manual therapy plays a smaller role here than in B1-B3 — the primary lesion is not restriction but excess translation, so mobilization is not the main tool; soft tissue work for secondary guarding only | Closed-chain and rhythmic stabilization exercises, proprioceptive/perturbation training, rotator cuff progressive loading, scapular dynamic control | Minimal |
| Phase 3 | Minimal | Minimal; hands-on time shifts to manual perturbation/resistance during dynamic exercises (a therapist-applied technique, not passive mobilization) | Progressive loading into the previously provocative position, sport/occupation-specific dynamic stability drills, plyometric/reactive training for higher-demand return-to-sport cases | Minimal |

> **Clinical Note:** Manual therapy's role in this protocol is fundamentally different from B1-B3 — the goal is never to increase mobility/translation, and the therapist should actively avoid any technique that stretches or loosens the capsuloligamentous structures already implicated in the instability. Where manual therapy is used, it is for secondary muscular guarding, not joint mobilization in the traditional sense.

### B4.6 Manual Therapy Detail

- **Phase 1:** Soft tissue release to guarding muscles (upper trapezius, levator scapulae, pectoralis major/minor) only — no joint mobilization directed at increasing glenohumeral translation or capsular length.
- **Phase 2-3:** Manual therapy is minimal and supportive; the therapist's most valuable manual contribution in this protocol is **manually resisted rhythmic stabilization** — applying unpredictable multi-directional perturbations to the humerus while the patient holds a static position, progressively challenging the dynamic stabilizers (rotator cuff, scapular musculature) to react and correct — this is a therapist-applied technique that belongs functionally with "manual therapy" time even though it is really a neuromuscular exercise technique.
- Do not apply Grade III-IV joint mobilization, sustained end-range capsular stretching, or MET aimed at increasing range in the direction of the patient's instability at any phase.

### B4.7 Exercise Prescription Detail

The exercise emphasis for this condition is **dynamic stability and proprioception**, not range restoration (which is rarely the deficit) or pure strength in isolation:

| Phase | Exercise focus | Example progressions |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Pain-free, protected-range activation; scapular setting; rotator cuff isometrics avoiding the provocative position | Isometric external/internal rotation at neutral/mid-range, scapular retraction holds, closed-chain weight-shifting on a stable surface (e.g. hands on a wall or table) within a comfortable range |
| Phase 2 | Closed-chain progressive loading, rhythmic stabilization, proprioceptive/perturbation training, rotator cuff isotonic loading | Quadruped/wall closed-chain stability holds with therapist- or self-applied perturbation, resistance-band external/internal rotation through increasing range, rhythmic stabilization drills, proprioceptive drills (e.g. reaching tasks with eyes closed, unstable surface work) |
| Phase 3 | Open-chain loading into the previously provocative position; sport/occupation-specific dynamic control; reactive/plyometric work for higher-demand return-to-sport | Progressive resistance training through full range including abduction-external rotation, PNF diagonal patterns (D2 flexion), plyometric drills (e.g. rebounder throws for overhead athletes), sport-specific technique retraining with a coach where relevant |

> **Clinical Note:** Closed-chain exercise (hand/arm fixed against a surface) is prioritized early because it provides inherent joint compression that assists dynamic stability (concavity-compression effect) with lower demand on isolated muscular control than open-chain work — progress to open-chain loading only once closed-chain control is confidently demonstrated.

- **Dosage principle specific to this condition:** progression is gated by demonstrated *quality of control* (no apprehension, no compensatory substitution, smooth perturbation-recovery) rather than by a fixed pain-based rule as in B2/B3 — pain is often not the limiting symptom in instability; loss of control or apprehension is the signal to regress.

### B4.8 Electrotherapy Detail

- Minimal role in this condition specifically — electrotherapy does not address translation, laxity, or dynamic control, the core deficits targeted here.
- TENS/hot pack may be used adjunctively for comfort and secondary muscular guarding in Phase 1, following the same general principles as B1-B3 (Module 2.5) — never used as a substitute for the dynamic stability exercise program, which is unambiguously the primary intervention for this condition.

### B4.9 Acupuncture / Dry Needling Detail

- Indicated only for secondary myofascial guarding/trigger points in periscapular muscles contributing to compensatory pain patterns, following the same enabling-role principle as B1-B3 (Module 2.5).
- Not indicated as a primary or notably beneficial intervention for the instability itself, since needling has no mechanism for improving static or dynamic joint stability.

### B4.10 Contraindications and Precautions

- **First-time traumatic anterior dislocation in a young (<25), high-demand or contact-sport athlete**: discuss early surgical stabilization with the referring orthopedic surgeon as a genuine first-line option rather than defaulting to prolonged conservative management — recurrence rates in this specific population are high with conservative management alone. This is a shared physician-patient decision; physiotherapy's role is to support whichever pathway is chosen, and to flag the patient's risk profile clearly in the referral note.
- Avoid any stretching, mobilization, or exercise technique that increases range or reduces tension in the direction of the patient's instability (e.g. do not perform posterior-capsule/sleeper stretching in an anteriorly-unstable shoulder without careful individual reasoning, unlike its routine use in B2/B3 GIRD management).
- For confirmed structural lesions (Bankart, significant Hill-Sachs) with recurrent instability despite an adequate conservative trial, refer for a surgical opinion (labral repair/stabilization) rather than continuing an open-ended strengthening program.
- Screen for and manage generalized hypermobility conditions (e.g. Ehlers-Danlos spectrum) as a modifying factor in prognosis and exercise dosing — these patients typically need a longer, more gradual loading progression and often benefit from broader kinetic-chain/postural conditioning, not an isolated shoulder-only program.
- Avoid aggressive early loading into the provocative position (abduction-external rotation for anterior instability) before Phase 3 criteria are met.

### B4.11 Progression Criteria

**Progress phase/loading level when:**
- Phase 1 → 2: Pain-free protected-range ROM is achieved and rotator cuff isometrics are well-tolerated without apprehension.
- Phase 2 → 3: Closed-chain dynamic stability tasks are performed with good control and no apprehension, including under therapist-applied perturbation; rotator cuff strength is trending toward symmetry.
- Within Phase 3, progress toward the provocative position/open-chain loading only when the patient demonstrates consistent, apprehension-free control at the current loading level across multiple sessions — not on a single good session.

**Regress or hold when:**
- Apprehension reappears at a previously-tolerated loading level or position.
- A subluxation/instability "event" occurs during the rehabilitation process (even a brief, self-reduced one reported by the patient) — this requires a full re-assessment before continuing to progress, not a simple return to the prior exercise level, since it may indicate a change in the underlying pathology or an inadequate program.
- Compensatory substitution patterns (e.g. excessive scapular hiking, trunk lean) reappear under load — reduce load/complexity until control is restored.

### B4.12 Red Flags Specific to This Condition

In addition to universal red flags (Module 1, 1.2):

- **Neurovascular compromise at the time of a dislocation event** (axillary nerve injury — deltoid weakness/regimental badge sensory loss — is the most common; also screen distal pulses) — this is an acute-injury red flag requiring immediate medical assessment, not a routine physiotherapy intake finding to simply document.
- **Recurrent dislocation with progressively lower-force triggering events** (e.g. dislocating during normal daily activity or sleep) — suggests significant structural compromise and should prompt surgical referral discussion rather than continued conservative management alone.
- **Multi-joint instability with systemic features** (unusually stretchy skin, poor wound healing, family history of connective tissue disorder) — consider referral to rule out a heritable connective tissue disorder (e.g. Ehlers-Danlos syndrome), which changes long-term management expectations significantly.

### B4.13 Home Exercise Program

| Phase | Home program emphasis | Example content |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Pain-free protected-range maintenance; education on positions to avoid | Gentle active-assisted ROM within the safe arc, scapular setting, clear written guidance on which positions/activities to avoid until cleared to progress |
| Phase 2 | Daily closed-chain stability and rotator cuff loading program | Wall/table closed-chain holds, resistance-band rotation exercises, home proprioceptive drills (e.g. weight-shifting, reaching tasks) |
| Phase 3 | Progressive home program building toward sport/occupation-specific demand; long-term maintenance mindset | Progressive resistance program through full range, sport-specific home drills, an explicit long-term (beyond-discharge) maintenance program given the elevated recurrence risk in this population |

- Educate the patient explicitly that instability — particularly AMBRI/MDI presentations — is managed as an ongoing conditioning need, not a short course that ends recurrence risk permanently; frame the home program as a long-term habit, especially for those returning to overhead sport or heavy manual work.
- For patients who opted for or underwent surgical stabilization, this B4 home program does not apply as-is post-operatively — follow the surgeon's specific post-stabilization protocol (cross-reference B27: Post-Surgical General Rehab Principles once written) and reintroduce this protocol's later-phase content only once the surgeon clears progressive loading.

### B4.14 Evidence Grade Summary

| Intervention | Evidence Level | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Closed-chain dynamic stability + proprioceptive exercise | Moderate-High | Particularly well-supported for atraumatic/multidirectional (AMBRI) presentations, the more physiotherapy-responsive population |
| Progressive open-chain loading (later phase) | Moderate | Gated by demonstrated quality of control rather than a fixed timeline (B4.7) |
| Surgical stabilization (traumatic anterior dislocation, young/high-demand athlete — TUBS-leaning) | Moderate-High for this subgroup | Lower recurrence risk than conservative management alone in first-time traumatic dislocation in young, high-demand patients; shared physician-patient decision |
| Electrotherapy | Low | Minimal role — does not address translation, laxity, or dynamic control, the core deficits in this condition |
| Dry needling (secondary myofascial guarding) | Low-Moderate | Adjunct only; no mechanism for improving joint stability itself |

### B4.15 Outcome Measures & Cutoffs

| Measure | What it captures | Clinically meaningful change (MCID) | When to administer |
|---|---|---|---|
| WOSI (Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index) | Instability-specific quality of life | Condition-specific cutoff — confirm current validated MCID against the source literature before quoting a fixed figure to a patient | Baseline, then every reassessment point |
