## B10. Low Back: Facet Joint Syndrome

> **Format note:** This protocol applies the standard Module 2.3 7-14-28 day phase framework to lumbar facet joint syndrome — a common mechanical low back pain presentation originating from the zygapophyseal (facet) joints. Facet joint pain is typically unilateral, extension- and rotation-provoked, and relieved by flexion. It is one of the most common specific sources of mechanical low back pain (often responsible for 15-30% of chronic LBP cases) and is frequently under-recognized due to its overlap with non-specific LBP (B8). This protocol is intended for patients whose facet joint involvement is the dominant clinical picture, rather than a minor component of a broader NSLBP presentation — use B8 as the primary protocol when facet findings are present but not clearly dominant.

### B10.1 Anatomy Link

The lumbar zygapophyseal (facet) joints are paired synovial joints between adjacent lumbar vertebrae (L1-L5, L5-S1). They serve three primary functions: (1) guiding vertebral motion, (2) resisting shear forces, and (3) limiting rotation and extension. Each facet joint is innervated by the medial branch of the dorsal ramus of the segmental spinal nerve, which provides the anatomical substrate for both pain referral and targeted treatment (e.g., medial branch blocks).

- **Orientation:** Lumbar facet joints are oriented in the sagittal plane (facing medial/lateral) in the upper lumbar spine, becoming more coronally oriented (facing anterior/posterior) at L5-S1. This orientation transition makes L4-5 and L5-S1 the most vulnerable to rotational and extension stress.
- **Capsular pattern:** The facet joint capsule is richly innervated with nociceptive fibers (more densely than most other lumbar structures), making it a sensitive source of mechanical pain. Facet joint pain is frequently associated with synovial inflammation, capsular distension, or mechanical impingement from osteophytes or meniscoid entrapment.
- **Biomechanical coupling:** Facet joint stress increases with: (a) lumbar extension (the joint surfaces approximate), (b) rotation (shear across the joint), and (c) prolonged sitting (increased intradiscal pressure + reduced lumbar lordosis, leading to altered facet loading). Flexion, conversely, opens the facet joints and typically reduces stress — hence the flexion-relief characteristic of facet-predominant pain.
- **Referred pain patterns:** The medial branch of the dorsal ramus provides segmental referral patterns that can be clinically useful:
  - L2-3 facet → pain referred to the upper buttock and lateral thigh
  - L3-4 facet → pain referred to the mid-buttock and lateral thigh
  - L4-5 facet → pain referred to the lower buttock, posterior thigh, and occasionally the calf (but does NOT extend below the knee — this is a key discriminator from discogenic radiculopathy, B9)
  - L5-S1 facet → pain referred to the gluteal region and posterior thigh

> **Clinical Note:** Facet joint pain is often overlooked because it mimics other low back conditions, particularly sacroiliac joint dysfunction (B11) and, to a lesser extent, discogenic pain (B9). The key discriminator is the provocative position: facet pain is consistently aggravated by extension and rotation, and relieved by flexion. If extension-relief is the predominant pattern (patient gets up from sitting by bending forward and feels better), discogenic pain (B9) or spinal stenosis is more likely; if flexion-relief is predominant, facet pain is more likely. This directional preference is the most clinically useful differentiator.

### B10.2 Assessment Clues

Building on Module 1's standard assessment and B8's NSLBP findings, with facet-joint-specific additions:

| Finding | Typical Facet Joint Syndrome pattern |
|---|---|
| Onset | Often insidious or related to a relatively minor trigger (prolonged sitting, sudden extension/rotation movement) rather than high-force trauma; may follow a specific incident of twisting, "standing up too fast," or "reaching back for something" |
| Location | Unilateral (or occasionally bilateral) paramedian low back pain, typically lateral to the midline, in the region of the facet joints, with referral into the buttock and posterior thigh — pain does NOT extend below the knee (this is the key discriminator from B9) |
| Pain behavior | Mechanical: provoked by extension (standing, walking, lying prone), rotation (twisting, turning in bed), and prolonged sitting; relieved by flexion (sitting with the back forward, leaning forward to wash hands, lying in a fetal position); pain is typically worse in the morning after getting out of bed (the joint has been in extension during sleep) and improves with movement, but may worsen again at the end of the day with prolonged activity |
| AROM | Restricted, especially extension and rotation to the symptomatic side; flexion is usually full or only mildly restricted; the most provocative movement is typically combined extension + rotation |
| PROM | Passive extension and rotation reproduce the patient's familiar pain; passive side-flexion away from the symptomatic side may also be provocative (stretching the facet capsule); central PA pressures may reproduce local pain but are often less specific than unilateral PA pressures |
| Unilateral PA pressures | Positive: reproducing the patient's familiar pain (not just local discomfort) at a specific level, typically L4-5 or L5-S1; this is the most specific manual assessment finding for facet joint pain — document the level(s) and the exact symptom reproduced |
| Extension-rotation test | Positive: reproduction of familiar pain when the patient actively extends and rotates the lumbar spine (standing extension + ipsilateral rotation to the symptomatic side) |
| Muscle testing | Lower extremity myotomes are full and symmetrical — the key discriminator against radiculopathy (B9); hip and core musculature may be weak/deconditioned (not neurologically weak) contributing to the movement pattern; gluteal muscles may be tight or weak |
| Neurological exam | Normal — no myotomal weakness, no dermatomal sensory loss, normal reflexes, negative SLR (or only producing hamstring tightness/posterior thigh pain, not radicular leg pain) |
| Special tests | SLR is negative (or produces buttock/thigh pain but NOT radicular leg pain); slump test may be mildly positive bilaterally but not reproducing unilateral radicular symptoms; facet provocation tests (extension + rotation, unilateral PA pressures) are the key positive findings |
| Gait | Typically not significantly impaired unless pain is severe; patients may have a slight forward-leaning posture to relieve extension stress; they often stand with a flexed trunk ("looking down"), not the straight-up posture of a discogenic pain patient |
| Posture | Often associated with increased lumbar lordosis (which increases facet loading) in extension-dominant patients, or a relatively normal lordosis with rotation asymmetry; thoracic kyphosis may be present |
| Correlation with imaging | X-ray may show facet joint hypertrophy, osteophytes, or joint space narrowing; MRI may show facet hypertrophy/edema or synovitis; however, imaging findings correlate poorly with symptoms in many cases — facet joint changes are equally common in asymptomatic individuals of the same age, so imaging alone should not drive the diagnosis |

> **Clinical Note:** The single most useful diagnostic cluster for facet joint pain consists of: (1) unilateral, paramedian pain, (2) pain reproduced on unilateral PA pressure at a specific level, (3) pain aggravated by extension/rotation and relieved by flexion, and (4) pain referral into the buttock/thigh but not below the knee. If three of these four are positive, the clinical diagnosis of facet joint syndrome is strongly supported. If the patient has a strong flexion-relief pattern and negative SLR, discogenic pain (B9) and SIJ dysfunction (B11) become less likely.

### B10.3 Differential Diagnosis

| Differential | Distinguishing feature against Facet Joint Syndrome |
|---|---|
| Non-specific LBP (B8) | Diffuse, often non-segmental pain; no clear unilateral segmental pattern; no specific extension-rotation reproduction; less clearly relieved by flexion; no unilateral PA pressure replication |
| Sciatica / Disc Prolapse (B9) | Pain radiates below the knee, follows a dermatomal pattern; positive SLR; neurological deficit (weakness, sensory loss, reflex changes); flexion is often provocative (discogenic) or extension-relief (stenosis), not flexion-relief |
| SIJ Dysfunction (B11) | Pain localized to the posterior pelvis, aggravated by sitting and rising; positive SIJ provocation tests (≥3 of: thigh thrust, Gaenslen's, distraction, compression, sacral thrust); unilateral PA pressure over the SIJ reproduces pain but does not reproduce a clear segmental (facet) level |
| Lumbar Spondylosis/Spondylolisthesis (B12) | Typically older patients (>50) for spondylosis; X-ray shows degenerative changes, disc space narrowing, or listhesis; often a mixed mechanical/radicular presentation; may co-exist with facet arthropathy (facet involvement is part of the spondylotic process) |
| Inflammatory Arthropathy (e.g. ankylosing spondylitis) | Marked morning stiffness (>1 hour), night pain unrelieved by position change, improvement with exercise, multiple joint involvement, systemic symptoms, younger male patient, family history, elevated ESR/CRP; facet involvement is part of the axial inflammation |
| Piriformis Syndrome | Buttock pain aggravated by sitting, deep palpation over the piriformis reproduces pain; no lumbar extension-rotation provocation; no unilateral PA pressure replication at a specific lumbar level |
| Hip Pathology (OA, gluteal tendinopathy) | Pain localized to the groin, anterior thigh, or greater trochanter; hip ROM restriction (especially internal rotation), not reproduced by lumbar extension-rotation; FADIR/FABER tests positive |
| Myofascial Pain | Pain is more regional than segmental; active trigger points in lumbar paraspinal muscles reproduce pain, but unilateral PA pressure does not reproduce a segmental pattern; often co-exists with facet pain as a secondary guarding phenomenon — distinguish by whether PA pressure at the level reproduces the primary pain (facet) or only local tenderness (myofascial) |

### B10.4 Phase Goals (7-14-28 Day Framework)

Facet joint syndrome follows the standard Module 2.3 phase structure, adapted for the flexion-relief, extension-avoidance pattern:

| Phase | Typical window | Primary aim | Key metrics to track |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 — Acute/Protective | Day 1-7 | Reduce pain, reduce irritability, identify and use flexion-easing positions, educate on activity modification to avoid extension/rotation, establish flexion-based exercises | NPRS trend (rest and movement); ability to identify and modify provocative extension/rotation postures; flexion-relief pattern established; patient understands the mechanical nature of the condition |
| Phase 2 — Sub-acute/Restorative | Day 7-14 | Establish flexion-based and neutral spine mobility exercises, improve lumbar segmental mobility (especially extension restriction), address associated hip and core tightness/weakness, begin graded extension reintroduction | Reduced pain during extension and rotation; improved lumbar flexibility; improved functional mobility; reduced muscle guarding/trigger point activity; improved sitting tolerance |
| Phase 3 — Remodeling/Functional | Day 14-28 | Build core and lumbopelvic endurance and strength, restore functional extension and rotation capacity, integrate into occupational/functional demands, return to full activity | Pain-free extension and rotation within functional range; return to work/sport/activity at a meaningful level; patient demonstrates self-efficacy in managing symptoms; independent postural self-management |
| Phase 4 — Discharge/Maintenance | Beyond Day 28 (chronic/recurrent cases, or slow-responders) | Independent self-management, relapse-prevention education (particularly extension-avoidance strategies), ongoing conditioning | Patient demonstrates independent home program; outcome measure (ODI or RMDQ) approaching normative score; patient has a clear, written flare-management plan for extension-avoidance |

- **Time windows:** Facet syndrome typically responds more rapidly to physiotherapy than discogenic pain, with most patients showing meaningful improvement within 1-2 weeks. However, chronic facet arthropathy with significant degenerative change may require a longer, maintenance-oriented approach.
- **Prognostic indicators for this condition:** A clear flexion-relief pattern, good response to unilateral PA mobilization in the first session, and absence of neurological signs all predict a favorable response to conservative care.

> **Clinical Note:** The most important principle in facet syndrome is that flexion is therapeutic and extension is provocative (in contrast to discogenic sciatica, where extension is often therapeutic and flexion provocative). However, this is not absolute — some patients may have a mixed picture, where flexion is still provocative but extension is more so. In such cases, the directional preference assessment (B10.7) is the guide: whichever direction produces centralization or symptom reduction is the primary exercise direction.

### B10.5 Session Time-Block Application

Applying the Module 2.4 template to facet joint syndrome, weighted by phase:

| Phase | Opening electro (~10 min) | Manual therapy (~10-12 min) | Exercise (~15-20 min) | Closing electro (~10 min) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Heat pack (if comfort improves mobility and is not contraindicated); TENS for pain modulation if high irritability; avoid heat if there is acute inflammation/tenderness | Gentle soft tissue release to lumbar paraspinal and gluteal muscles, quadratus lumborum, and gluteus medius (these are commonly tight/guarded in facet pain); Grade I-II unilateral PA oscillations to the symptomatic segment(s) in a pain-free or minimally symptomatic position; avoid extension mobilization or aggressive rotation; identify directional preference; may perform gentle flexion-based MET (contract-relax for lumbar flexion) if it eases symptoms | Flexion-based exercises: knee-to-chest in supine (single then double), supine pelvic tilt, sidelying fetal position; gentle lumbar mobility in the flexion direction; walking as tolerated (but avoid prolonged standing or extension); postural/ergonomic education (workstation setup, sitting posture, avoiding extension postures) | Ice (if acute/inflammatory) or TENS (if irritability remains) |
| Phase 2 | Heat pack before manual therapy/exercise (to relax guarding and improve tissue extensibility) | Progress to Grade III-IV mobilization to restricted segments, particularly in flexion (e.g., active-assisted or passive mobilization into flexion to improve the restricted movement in this direction); unilateral PA pressures at the symptomatic level(s) — progress to Grade III as irritability settles; MET for lumbar rotation restriction; soft tissue release to trigger points; SIJ mobilization if SIJ dysfunction is a significant contributor (B11 considerations) | Progress flexion-based exercises; introduce neutral spine mobility (cat-camel but avoiding extension if it provokes); core stabilization (dead bug, bird-dog, but with flexion-biased or neutral-spine positioning); gentle extension reintroduction (graded, monitored for symptom response); hip mobility work (gluteal stretching, piriformis stretch) | Minimal; optional ice if exercise provoked soreness |
| Phase 3 | Minimal/optional; shift to exercise-dominant sessions | Maintenance mobilization only for residual restrictions; hands-on time shifts to guided exercise form, functional training, and active coaching | Progressive core and lumbopelvic strengthening; functional extension and rotation training (graded, monitored); sport-specific retraining; graded return to provocative activities; postural endurance work | Minimal; taper in favor of exercise-dominant care |

> **Clinical Note on Directional Preference:** In facet syndrome, the directional preference is typically flexion. The first session's key task is to identify that flexion reduces symptoms and to teach the patient to use flexion-based positions for symptom relief (e.g., knee-to-chest, fetal position, leaning forward). If no clear directional preference exists, use neutral spine/activity-pacing approach (similar to B8's no-preference protocol). If extension is unexpectedly therapeutic (symptoms reduce with extension), reconsider the diagnosis toward B9 (discogenic) or B12 (stenosis).

### B10.6 Manual Therapy Detail

Manual therapy is the primary physical intervention for facet syndrome, with a strong evidence base for segmental mobilization and MET:

- **Phase 1:** Maitland Grade I-II oscillatory mobilizations — unilateral PA pressures — applied to the symptomatic segment(s) identified in assessment (most commonly L4-5 or L5-S1). Technique: oscillate at 2-3 Hz, low amplitude at the onset of resistance (not at end-range), pain-relief/irritability-reduction grade. Soft tissue release to lumbar paraspinal muscles, quadratus lumborum, gluteal muscles (especially gluteus medius), and piriformis — palpate for trigger points/tension; apply ischemic compression for 30-60 seconds to active trigger points if not too irritable; if very irritable, use gentle sustained pressure instead. Gentle flexion-based MET: with the patient in supine, gently flex the lumbar spine while the patient resists a small extension force (contract-relax in the direction of flexion), to promote relaxation and improve flexion range — but ensure this does not provoke pain (it should reduce it). Do not perform high-velocity thrust manipulation on the first visit until the patient's irritability and suitability is fully assessed, and do not perform aggressive extension mobilization in the acute phase.
- **Phase 2:** Progress to Grade III-IV mobilizations — larger amplitude oscillations reaching into the tissue resistance/restriction (end-range, but not pushing into sharp pain). Apply unilateral PA pressures at the segment(s) most restricted (reduced mobility on passive segmental testing). For facet syndrome, this typically involves increasing the PA force in a posterior-to-anterior direction (pushing the spinous process anteriorly, which opens the facet joints) — but the exact direction depends on the specific restriction. MET for lumbar rotation and side-flexion restriction: contract-relax with gentle pre-positioning into restriction, 5-second hold, 30-50% effort, repeat 3-5 times with increasing pre-positioning. For rotation restriction: the patient is positioned in side-lying, the therapist pre-positions the patient into rotation toward the restricted side, the patient resists a gentle force in the opposite direction, then relaxes into the new position. SIJ mobilization if SIJ dysfunction is identified as a significant contributor (cross-reference B11 for detailed technique). Soft tissue release to secondary guarding muscles continues.
- **Phase 3:** Manual therapy tapers to maintenance and residual restriction targeting; the therapist's hands-on time increasingly shifts to guiding exercise form, particularly reinforcing correct flexion-pattern and core activation during functional exercises. If the patient is still experiencing a mild residual extension restriction, focused Grade III-IV flexion mobilization to the most restricted segment can be performed as needed.

> **Clinical Note on Unilateral PA Pressures:** The most specific manual technique for facet syndrome is unilateral PA pressure at the symptomatic level. The therapist positions the thumb (or reinforced finger) lateral to the spinous process on the symptomatic side and applies a posterior-to-anterior (PA) force. If this reproduces the patient's familiar pain, it strongly supports a facet source at that level. Grade I-II oscillations (pain-relief grade) are used in Phase 1; Grade III-IV (end-range) are used in Phase 2. Central PA pressures (over the spinous process) may also be useful but are generally less specific for the facet joint than unilateral pressures.

### B10.7 Exercise Prescription Detail

Exercise for facet syndrome emphasizes flexion-biased movement, segmental mobility, and core stabilization, with gradual reintroduction of extension:

| Phase | Exercise focus | Example progressions |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Flexion-based exercises in the pain-easing direction; gentle pain-free mobility; walking as tolerated; postural and ergonomic education | Knee-to-chest in supine (single then double) — start with 5-10 reps, 3-5 times daily; supine pelvic tilt (gently flattening the lower back against the floor); sidelying fetal position for symptom relief (held for 30-60 seconds, repeated); gentle seated flexion ("sitting slump" with the back rounded) if this eases symptoms; walking as tolerated (but avoid standing still for long periods, as this extends the lumbar spine). Postural correction: sit with lumbar support, avoid slumping backward into extension; teach the patient to lean forward slightly during sitting to maintain flexion-relief; avoid lying prone |
| Phase 2 | Progress flexion-based exercises; introduce neutral spine mobility (cat-camel with an emphasis on the cat/flexion phase); core stabilization; graded extension reintroduction; hip mobility | Flexion progression: standing forward flexion (if tolerated), seated flexion (longer holds), double knee-to-chest with rocking. Neutral spine mobility: cat-camel — but with emphasis on the flexion phase of the movement, avoiding extension if it provokes; perform gentle, controlled movements. Core stabilization: dead bug (arms/legs), bird-dog, side-lying plank (regressed to modified plank if needed), abdominal bracing during daily activities. Graded extension reintroduction: standing extension in small increments (e.g., standing, hands on hips, slowly extend backward while monitoring for pain — stop at the first sign of discomfort and regress). Hip mobility: kneeling hip flexor stretch, supine glute stretch (for gluteus medius/piriformis), standing hamstring stretch |
| Phase 3 | Progressive core and lumbopelvic strengthening; full functional extension and rotation training; sport-specific retraining; return to full activity | Core strengthening progression: full plank, side plank, dead bug with resistance, bird-dog with resistance, anti-rotation drills (Pallof press). Extension and rotation functional training: graded standing extension progressing to full range; graded rotational exercises (e.g., trunk rotation with a stick, golf swing without a club) with core stabilization cueing. Functional training: squat/lunge/deadlift patterns with postural cueing (maintaining a neutral spine with a slight flexion bias), loaded carrying (farmer's carry), lifting technique practice (moderate loads with perfect form). Endurance training: graded walking program (with an emphasis on maintaining pelvic neutral/slight flexion), cycling (if flexion-tolerated). Sport-specific work: retraining golf swing, tennis serve, bowling, or other sport-specific postures with core stabilization cueing and graded extension-rotation loading |

- **Dosage principle for facet syndrome:** Start with low volume (e.g., 5-10 reps, 2-3 sets, 2-3 times daily for flexion exercises; 3-5 sets of 8-15 reps for strengthening) and progress based on symptom response. Exercise should produce a tolerable discomfort (≤3/10 pain that settles within an hour) — the "sweet spot" for recovery. Complete avoidance of extension (which is the provocative direction) delays adaptation; gradual reintroduction is the key to full functional recovery.
- **Pain-monitoring rule:** Pain during exercise is acceptable up to 3/10 NPRS if it settles promptly. However, for this condition, the location of pain is as important as the intensity: if extension exercise causes pain to move to the buttock/thigh (peripheralize), stop and regress. If extension exercise causes only local, low-level discomfort that settles within an hour, it is acceptable and may be a sign of desensitization.
- **Walking prescription:** Walking is accessible and beneficial, but patients with facet syndrome should be advised to (a) avoid standing still or walking on hard surfaces for long periods (extension stress), (b) take breaks to sit or lean forward, (c) walk with a slight forward-leaning posture to avoid extension.

> **Clinical Note on Extension Reintroduction:** Complete avoidance of extension is not the goal; the goal is gradual reintroduction after the acute irritability has settled. Extension is a normal and necessary movement for functional activities (e.g., reaching overhead, washing hair, lying prone). The therapist should progressively guide the patient from flexion-dominant exercises in Phase 1 toward a full functional range of movement that includes all directions, with extension included in a graded, pain-monitored manner in Phase 2-3. A patient who is discharged still avoiding extension is not fully rehabilitated and is at risk of recurrence when they inevitably need to extend.

### B10.8 Electrotherapy Detail

- **TENS:** Indicated for symptomatic pain relief in Phase 1, particularly for patients with high irritability or those who are not yet engaging effectively with active exercises due to pain. Conventional TENS (high-frequency, 80-120 Hz, low-intensity) applied to the lumbar paraspinal region — teach the patient home-use TENS if night pain or prolonged sitting pain is a barrier to sleep/work function.
- **Heat pack:** Useful before manual therapy and exercise in Phase 1-2 to relax guarding and improve tissue extensibility — a routine precursor to lumbar mobilization and flexion-based exercises. Avoid if there is acute inflammation/swelling (first 24-48 hours post-acute injury).
- **Ice:** If there is an acute inflammatory component (significant pain flare, swelling, severe irritability), ice applied to the lumbar region for 15-20 minutes, 2-3 times daily, may provide symptomatic relief in the first 24-48 hours. Use ice rather than heat if the patient is highly irritable.
- **Therapeutic ultrasound / IFT:** Weak evidence for facet joint syndrome specifically — may be used as an adjunct for pain relief and muscle relaxation if the clinic includes it, but never substitutes for manual therapy or exercise time.
- **Laser/LLLT:** Some evidence for short-term pain relief in facet-mediated pain, but current evidence does not support it as a primary driver of recovery — use only as an adjunct where it is available and the patient responds symptomatically.

### B10.9 Acupuncture / Dry Needling Detail

- Indicated for myofascial trigger points in the lumbar paraspinal muscles (erector spinae, multifidus, quadratus lumborum), gluteal muscles (gluteus medius, gluteus minimus), and piriformis, which are common in facet syndrome and contribute significantly to the pain and guarding pattern.
- Particularly useful for trigger points in the quadratus lumborum (often palpated laterally in the lumbar region) and gluteus medius (a common source of referral pain to the lateral hip and buttock) — these muscles are frequently tight and guarded in response to facet joint irritation and respond well to needling.
- Rationale: reducing myofascial guarding can improve pain-free lumbar mobility, reduce referral symptoms, and allow more effective manual therapy and exercise in the same or following session — consistent with Module 2.5's acupuncture principle of enabling primary interventions.
- Not indicated as a primary treatment for the facet joint pathology itself — it addresses the muscular component only, which is often secondary; if segmental mobility testing demonstrates a clear facet joint restriction, joint mobilization is the primary treatment; dry needling alone is insufficient.
- *(Full needle selection, depth, technique, and safety precautions for each lumbar/gluteal muscle will be detailed in Module 6: Acupuncture/Dry Needling Protocol Library.)*

### B10.10 Contraindications and Precautions

- **Lumbar manipulation (high-velocity thrust):** This is a moderate-risk intervention with potential for serious complications. At Relife, lumbar manipulation is performed only by clinicians specifically trained and certified in spinal manipulation technique, and only after a full pre-manipulative safety screen (red flags, B10.12, and imaging where indicated). For facet syndrome specifically, thrust manipulation may be appropriate in the later phases (after acute irritability has settled) but is not a first-line treatment. For non-certified clinicians, use Maitland mobilizations (Grade I-IV) as the primary manual therapy technique and do not substitute with thrust manipulation.
- **Acute radiculopathy (B9) red flags** — if a patient develops neurological symptoms (radiating leg pain below the knee, paresthesia, weakness) at any point during care, they should be reclassified to B9 and managed according to that protocol, not continued under the facet syndrome label.
- **Cauda equina red flags** — saddle anesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, bilateral leg weakness, progressive neurological deficit — absolute contraindication to lumbar manual therapy; refer immediately for emergency assessment.
- **Osteoporosis** — use lower-grade mobilizations (Grade I-II) and avoid high-velocity thrust manipulation; patients with known osteoporosis and lumbar pain should be managed with exercise and low-intensity manual techniques only.
- **Vertebral fracture** — a history of significant trauma, prolonged steroid use, or osteoporosis with sudden-onset severe pain are red flags; imaging is warranted before manual therapy.
- **Inflammatory arthritis (e.g. ankylosing spondylitis)** — a relative contraindication to high-velocity lumbar manipulation; use only gentle, low-grade mobilization techniques and coordinate with the patient's rheumatologist if manipulation is considered.
- **Extension mobilization timing:** Avoid aggressive extension mobilization or over-extension in the acute phase (Phase 1) — this can exacerbate the facet joint inflammation and worsen pain. Extension mobilization should be introduced gradually only after irritability has settled (Phase 2), and even then, it should be monitored for symptom response.
- **Rotational loading:** Avoid loaded rotation (e.g., golf swing, tennis serve, heavy twisting movements) until Phase 3, when the facet joint has been sufficiently desensitized and core stabilization is adequate to support controlled rotational movements.

### B10.11 Progression Criteria

Condition-specific thresholds, applied within the Module 2.7 generic framework:

**Progress to the next phase/loading level when:**

- Phase 1 → 2: NPRS at rest reduced to ≤3/10; flexion-based directional preference identified and correctly performed; patient can maintain a flexion-relief posture without symptom provocation; walking program established; patient understands extension-avoidance and flexion-relief principles.
- Phase 2 → 3: Extension and rotation pain has substantially reduced (NPRS ≤3/10 during these movements); segmental mobility improved (passive flexion and rotation are less restricted); core stabilization exercises performed with good form; functional mobility improved (sitting >30 minutes without significant pain, standing >30 minutes without extension pain).
- Within Phase 3, progress load/complexity when current exercise load is tolerated with ≤3/10 exercise pain that settles within 1 hour, and no >24h flare.

**Regress or hold when:**

- Pain during exercise exceeds 3/10 or persists >1 hour post-exercise; or a >24h flare occurs (return to the prior tolerated load level).
- New neurological symptoms appear (radiating leg pain, paresthesia, weakness) — halt lumbar mobilization and re-classify toward B9/neurological referral immediately.
- Extension or rotation symptoms worsen with a specific exercise — reduce or stop that movement direction and return to flexion-based exercises.
- Extension or rotation ROM worsens on reassessment — screen for a new aggravating factor or alternate diagnosis before continuing.

### B10.12 Red Flags Specific to This Condition

In addition to the universal red flags (Module 1, 1.2) and those listed in B8.12 and B9.12:

| Red Flag Category | Specific Indicators | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Coexisting Radiculopathy | New or worsening radiating leg pain below the knee, myotomal weakness, dermatomal sensory loss, positive SLR, reflex changes | Reclassify to B9 (Sciatica/Disc Prolapse) — do not continue treating as pure facet syndrome |
| Cauda Equina Syndrome | Saddle anesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, bilateral leg weakness, progressive neurological deficit | Immediate emergency referral — do not perform lumbar manual therapy |
| Vertebral Fracture | Significant trauma, prolonged steroid use, age >70 with minor trauma, sudden severe pain | Urgent imaging and orthopedic referral |
| Inflammatory Arthropathy | Marked morning stiffness (>1 hour), night pain, improvement with exercise, multiple joint involvement, systemic symptoms | Rheumatology referral; avoid aggressive manual therapy until diagnosis clarified |
| Progressive Symptoms | Facet symptoms worsening despite appropriate Phase 1/2 treatment | Reconsider differential diagnosis (B10.3) and escalate imaging/referral |
| No Improvement by Day 14 | No meaningful improvement in pain or function despite appropriate treatment | Consider diagnostic facet joint injection (medical management, not physiotherapy-delivered) to confirm the facet source and guide further care; if positive, consider radiofrequency neurotomy (referral to pain specialist) |

### B10.13 Home Exercise Program

| Phase | Home program emphasis | Example content |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Flexion-based exercises several times daily; extension-avoidance strategies; posture/ergonomic correction | Knee-to-chest (single then double) 5-10 reps, 3-5 times daily; supine pelvic tilt; sidelying fetal position for symptom relief; seated flexion (leaning forward) for symptom relief; avoid prolonged standing and extension postures; workplace ergonomics: sit with lumbar support, lean forward slightly during sitting, avoid slumping backward; "take a break every 20-30 minutes" guidance |
| Phase 2 | Structured daily flexion program; core stabilization; graded extension reintroduction; hip mobility | Flexion progression: standing forward flexion (if tolerated), seated flexion (longer holds), double knee-to-chest with rocking. Core stabilization: dead bug, bird-dog, side-lying plank (modified), abdominal bracing during daily activities. Graded extension: standing extension in small increments (monitoring for pain, stopping at the first sign of discomfort). Hip mobility: kneeling hip flexor stretch, supine glute stretch, standing hamstring stretch. Walking progression: increase by 5 minutes every few days, but avoid standing still for long periods |
| Phase 3 | Progressive home strengthening program; functional and occupational retraining; long-term relapse prevention | Core progression: full plank, side plank, Pallof press (if band available). Functional training: squat/lunge/deadlift patterns with form cueing, loaded carrying. Extension and rotation training: graded extension, graded rotational exercises (e.g., trunk rotation with a stick), sport-specific drills with core cueing. Endurance training: graded walking/hiking program |

- **Most important patient education messages for this condition:**
  1. "Your pain is coming from the small joints in your back (facet joints), not from a disc problem. These joints are irritated when you lean back or twist. Bending forward gives them relief."
  2. "Bending forward is therapeutic for you, not harmful — use it to relieve pain. Lean forward, do knee-to-chest exercises, and avoid leaning back."
  3. "The goal is not to avoid extension forever — we want to gradually desensitize the joint so you can move in all directions pain-free. The home program will guide you through this."
  4. "If you develop any new leg pain, numbness, or weakness, contact us immediately — this could indicate a disc problem instead of a facet problem."
- **Flare management plan:** provide every patient with a written plan for managing future flares: (1) resume flexion-based exercises; (2) use fetal position for symptom relief; (3) avoid extension and rotation; (4) use heat/ice/TENS for comfort; (5) if symptoms persist >48 hours or new neurological symptoms appear, contact the clinic.
- **Address workplace ergonomics explicitly:** desk setup, chair with lumbar support (but lean forward slightly), monitor height, regular breaks, avoid prolonged standing. Many patients have sedentary jobs and prolonged extension (leaning back in a chair) is a major aggravator — optimize the workstation with a slight forward lean or use a cushion/backrest to maintain flexion-relief.
- **Address sleep posture:** sidelying with a pillow between knees (fetal position is best for facet syndrome) or supine with pillows under the knees (which flexes the hips and reduces lumbar extension) — avoid prone lying (which extends the back).
- **Address lifting technique:** teach "sit-to-stand" lifting (hinge at the hips, keep the back straight, engage the core, keep the load close to the body) — practice it with light loads in the clinic so the patient can do it safely at home; emphasize avoiding extension while lifting.
