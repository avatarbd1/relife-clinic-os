# PART A — FOUNDATION MODULES

## Module 1: Patient Assessment

This module defines the standard assessment sequence applied to every new OPD musculoskeletal patient at Relife Physiotherapy Center, regardless of diagnosis. Condition-specific protocols in Part B reference these sections rather than repeating them, so this module must be followed consistently by all treating therapists.

> **Clinical Note:** A complete assessment should take 15–25 minutes for a straightforward case and up to 40 minutes for a complex or multi-joint presentation. Never skip Red Flag Screening to save time.

### 1.1 Subjective Assessment (History Taking)

The subjective examination establishes the clinical picture before any hands-on testing and guides which objective tests are necessary. Use the structured sequence below for every patient.

| Component | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Patient Profile | Age, occupation, dominant hand/leg, activity level, sport/hobbies |
| Chief Complaint | Patient's own words; site, onset (sudden/gradual), duration |
| History of Present Illness | Mechanism of injury or insidious onset; aggravating/easing factors; behavior over 24 hours (morning stiffness, night pain) |
| Pain Behavior | Constant vs intermittent; nature (sharp, dull, burning, radiating); referral pattern |
| Past Medical History | Previous episodes, surgeries, comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, osteoporosis, autoimmune disease) |
| Medication History | Analgesics, steroids, anticoagulants — relevant to manual therapy and electrotherapy safety |
| Functional Impact | Effect on work, ADLs, sleep, sport |
| Patient Goals/Expectations | What the patient wants to return to — drives goal-setting in Module 2 |

Use open-ended questions first ("Tell me what happened"), then narrow with closed questions to confirm specifics. Document in the patient's own words where possible for medico-legal clarity.

### 1.2 Red Flag Screening

Red flags must be screened in every subjective assessment before proceeding to physical examination. A positive red flag warrants medical referral before or alongside physiotherapy, not silent continuation of treatment.

| System | Red Flag Indicators |
|---|---|
| Cancer / Systemic | Unexplained weight loss, history of malignancy, night pain unrelieved by rest/position, age >50 with new onset back pain |
| Cauda Equina (spinal) | Saddle anesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, bilateral leg weakness, progressive neurological deficit |
| Infection | Fever, chills, recent infection, IV drug use, immunosuppression, night sweats |
| Fracture | Significant trauma, prolonged steroid use, osteoporosis, age >70 with minor trauma |
| Vascular | Chest pain, unexplained calf swelling/tenderness (DVT), pulsatile abdominal mass |
| Cervical/Vertebral Artery | Drop attacks, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, 5 D's and 3 N's (dizziness, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, drop attacks, nausea, nystagmus, numbness) |
| Inflammatory | Morning stiffness >1 hour, multiple joint involvement, systemic symptoms (suggestive of inflammatory arthropathy) |

> **Clinical Note:** If any red flag is positive, document clearly, inform the supervising clinician, and refer to the appropriate physician before initiating physiotherapy treatment. Continue with caution only for clearly unrelated, low-risk complaints.

### 1.3 Pain Assessment

Pain is quantified and qualified at the initial visit and re-assessed at every subsequent session to track progress objectively.

- Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS 0–10): at rest, on movement, and worst in 24 hours
- Pain location: body chart marking with site, radiation, and depth (superficial vs deep)
- Pain quality: sharp, dull, aching, burning, shooting, throbbing, tingling
- Pain pattern: constant vs intermittent; mechanical (movement-related) vs chemical/inflammatory (rest pain, night pain)
- Aggravating and easing factors with specific movements or positions
- Irritability: how much activity provokes pain, and how long it takes to settle — this determines how vigorously you can assess and treat

Classify the pain mechanism where possible — nociceptive, neuropathic, or nociplastic — as this changes treatment selection and patient education approach (see Module 2).

### 1.4 Range of Motion (ROM) Assessment

ROM is assessed actively first, then passively, and compared bilaterally.

1. **Active Range of Motion (AROM):** patient-generated movement; screens willingness to move, pain response, and movement quality/compensation patterns.
2. **Passive Range of Motion (PROM):** therapist-generated movement with the patient relaxed; differentiates contractile (muscle/tendon) from non-contractile (capsule/ligament) tissue when compared against AROM and resisted testing.
3. **End-feel assessment:** classify as normal (bony, capsular, soft-tissue approximation) or abnormal (empty, spasm, springy block, boggy) — critical for differentiating capsular restriction from muscle guarding.
4. **Goniometric measurement:** record in degrees using standard bony landmarks for the joint being tested; always compare to the unaffected side.

> **Clinical Note:** A capsular pattern (a joint-specific proportional restriction, e.g. shoulder: external rotation > abduction > internal rotation) suggests arthrogenic involvement and should prompt joint mobilization consideration in Module 3.

### 1.5 Muscle Testing (Manual Muscle Testing — MMT)

Manual Muscle Testing grades strength of a target muscle or muscle group against resistance, using the Oxford/MRC 0–5 scale.

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 — Normal | Full ROM against gravity, holds against maximal resistance |
| 4 — Good | Full ROM against gravity, holds against moderate resistance |
| 3 — Fair | Full ROM against gravity only, no added resistance tolerated |
| 2 — Poor | Full ROM with gravity eliminated (gravity-minimized position) |
| 1 — Trace | Visible or palpable muscle contraction, no joint movement |
| 0 — Zero | No palpable or visible contraction |

Test in a standardized position for each muscle, stabilize proximally to isolate the target muscle, and apply resistance through available range with a consistent, gradual build-up of force. Document pain provoked during resisted testing separately from strength grade, as it indicates a contractile lesion.

### 1.6 Neurological Examination

Performed whenever there is suspicion of nerve root, peripheral nerve, or central nervous system involvement (e.g. radiating pain, numbness, weakness disproportionate to a musculoskeletal pattern).

- Myotomes: resisted isometric testing at key levels (C5–T1 for upper limb, L2–S1 for lower limb) to localize nerve root involvement
- Dermatomes: light touch and pinprick sensation mapped against standard dermatomal charts
- Deep Tendon Reflexes: biceps (C5/6), brachioradialis (C6), triceps (C7) for upper limb; patellar (L3/4), Achilles (S1) for lower limb — graded 0 (absent) to 4+ (hyperreflexic/clonus)
- Upper Motor Neuron screening: Babinski, clonus, Hoffmann's sign when central involvement is suspected
- Neurodynamic/neural tension tests: Straight Leg Raise (SLR), Slump Test, Upper Limb Neurodynamic Tests (ULNT) — detailed under Special Tests

> **Clinical Note:** Any objective neurological deficit (true myotomal weakness, dermatomal sensory loss, or reflex change) should be documented precisely with grade/level and discussed with the referring or supervising clinician, particularly if progressive.

### 1.7 Special Tests

Special (orthopedic) tests are used to support or refute a clinical hypothesis generated from the subjective history and general physical findings. No single special test is diagnostic in isolation — interpret in clusters alongside history and other objective findings.

- Select tests based on the working hypothesis from history, not as a blanket battery for every patient
- Record each test as positive, negative, or equivocal, with the specific reproduced symptom noted (e.g. "familiar posterior shoulder pain" vs "non-specific discomfort")
- Condition-specific special tests are detailed within each condition protocol in Part B (e.g. Lachman's and McMurray's under Knee conditions, Hawkins-Kennedy and Neer's under Shoulder Impingement)

### 1.8 Functional Assessment

Functional assessment translates impairment-level findings into real-world activity limitations and provides a baseline for measuring meaningful recovery.

- Observation of functional tasks relevant to the complaint: gait, squat, stair negotiation, overhead reach, lifting, sitting-to-standing
- Movement quality and compensation patterns during functional tasks, not just whether the task is completed
- Work and sport-specific demands matched against current capacity
- Balance and proprioception screening where relevant (e.g. lower limb injuries, post-surgical, elderly patients)

### 1.9 Outcome Measures

Standardized, validated outcome measures are recorded at initial assessment, at clinically relevant intervals during care, and at discharge. They provide objective evidence of progress for the patient, the clinic record, and referring physicians.

| Region/Condition | Recommended Outcome Measure |
|---|---|
| Low Back Pain | Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) or Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire |
| Neck Pain | Neck Disability Index (NDI) |
| Shoulder | Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI) or DASH |
| Knee | Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) or Lysholm Score |
| Lower Limb (general) | Lower Extremity Functional Scale (LEFS) |
| General/Any Region | Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS), NPRS, Global Rating of Change |

Choose the measure that matches the primary region of complaint at intake and use the same measure consistently through the episode of care to track meaningful change.

### 1.10 Clinical Reasoning

Clinical reasoning integrates subjective and objective findings into a working diagnosis and a justified treatment plan. Relife therapists are expected to be able to articulate this reasoning explicitly, not just arrive at a treatment choice.

1. Generate hypotheses early from the subjective history (pattern recognition) and refine them as objective findings are gathered.
2. Use the SIN (Severity, Irritability, Nature) framework to gauge how aggressively to assess and load the tissue in the first session.
3. Differentiate mechanical/musculoskeletal presentations from those requiring medical referral (see Red Flag Screening, 1.2).
4. Correlate objective findings (ROM, MMT, neuro, special tests) — a credible diagnosis should have at least two to three congruent findings, not one isolated positive test.
5. State the working clinical diagnosis, list relevant differentials, and document the reasoning that ruled them in or out (cross-reference Module 2: Clinical Management).
